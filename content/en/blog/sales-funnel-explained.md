Search "what is a sales funnel" and most answers come from companies that sell marketing software or online courses, so they describe opt-in pages, long email sequences, upsells and a red countdown timer. A service business sells differently. The sale happens in a conversation, on the phone, at a site visit or in a meeting, so its funnel has one job: bring the right people to that conversation and lose as few of them as possible on the way.

## What is a sales funnel?

**A sales funnel is the path a stranger follows to become a client, from first hearing of you to paying you, and it narrows at every step because some people drop out.** A hundred people read your page, ten get in touch, five speak to you, two sign: drawn as a picture, that is a funnel, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom.

A funnel is not a piece of software. Every business with clients already has one, even if nobody designed it: a referral, a look at the website, a phone call, a quote. My own clients arrive through three entrances, referrals, cold emails and search, and all three end the same way, in a conversation.

Designing a funnel means deciding on purpose what happens at each step, then counting how many people reach the next one.

## The four stages: top, middle and bottom of the funnel

Marketers use several sets of names for the stages. The plainest is awareness, interest, decision and action, grouped into the top, the middle and the bottom of the funnel.

| Stage | Part | What the prospect does | What you provide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Top | Has a problem, searches, asks around | A Google result, a Google Business Profile, a referral, a cold email |
| Interest | Middle | Reads, compares, checks you are real | One page per service, prices or price ranges, examples of work, reviews |
| Decision | Bottom | Chooses who to contact | A phone number to tap, a short form, a booking link |
| Action | Bottom | Gets in touch, then says yes | A fast reply, a call or a visit, a clear quote, a follow-up |

For a service business, the action at the bottom is rarely a purchase on the website. It is a booked call, a site visit or a quote request, and the sale comes after. The stretch from first visit to booked call is the part your website controls, so it is the part this guide focuses on.

The decision stage is also where appearances count. When I design a site, I want the client's prospects to take the business seriously from the first screen; a business that looks established tends to be haggled with less, too.

## A sales funnel example: an accountant, from Google search to booked call

Here is an illustration: a fictional two-person accountancy practice in Leeds that wants more landlords as clients. The same steps work for an installer, a solicitor or a cleaning company.

1. **Search.** A landlord with three flats types "accountant for landlords Leeds". The practice appears on the map through its Google Business Profile, and in the results below with a page about landlords.
2. **Service page.** The click lands on that page, not on the home page. It says who the service is for, what is included, a price range or what the price depends on, and how the first conversation works.
3. **Next step.** The page ends with one clear choice: a phone number you can tap, or a link to book a 15-minute call. A three-field form sits underneath for people who prefer to write.
4. **Reply.** The landlord fills in the form at 9 pm. An alert reaches the accountant's phone, and the reply goes out before 9.30 the next morning, with a booking link.
5. **Call.** Fifteen minutes on what the landlord needs, what it costs and what happens next. This is where the sale really happens.
6. **Proposal.** A short written quote the same day, with a start date and the list of documents to send.
7. **Follow-up.** No answer after three working days, so a short email or a call asks whether anything is unclear. Many quotes that look lost were simply forgotten.

For an installer, step 5 becomes a site visit and the quote follows the visit; the shape does not change. Writing the steps down gives each one an owner and a deadline. "We'll get back to you" is not a step; "a reply before 9.30 the next working day" is.

## The B2B variant: when the funnel starts with a cold email

When you sell to businesses, you do not have to wait for a search. You choose the companies and write to them first. The rest of the funnel stays the same: they look at your website, then they get in touch.

I run this kind of funnel for Pilotech, an air conditioning installer near Paris (see the {{< a page="/projects/pilotech-teknopoint" >}}Teknopoint project{{< /a >}}). The campaigns went to three audiences: Paris restaurants, Paris offices and shops, and architects, who were the main focus. Each sequence had three short plain-text emails at most, no links, the founder's phone number and an invitation to look at the catalogue on the website. I do everything in these campaigns except replying: the replies go straight to the client.

Two lessons came out of it. Many prospects, architects above all, called instead of replying, because a call fits a busy day better than a written answer. And the third email often brought the positive answers: people were busy, not uninterested. In my campaigns the first responses usually arrive within 36 hours; over a year, the ones for Pilotech brought 15 new clients. My {{< a key="b2b-cold-emailing-guide" >}}B2B cold emailing guide{{< /a >}} covers the set-up and the rules for writing to companies.

## The numbers to watch at each stage

You do not need a dashboard. One row per month in a spreadsheet is enough, with a count for each stage.

| Stage | What to count | Where to find it |
|---|---|---|
| Visits | Visits to your service pages, by channel | Google Search Console (clicks), Google Analytics 4 |
| Enquiries | Calls, forms and bookings | Your inbox and call log, key events in GA4, calls in Google Business Profile |
| Conversations | Enquirers you actually spoke to, and how fast you replied | Your call log, or a column in the spreadsheet |
| Quotes | Proposals sent | Your quoting or invoicing tool |
| Clients | Quotes accepted, by first channel | The same spreadsheet |

Two labels help here: GA4 now calls conversions "key events", and Google Business Profile counts taps on your call button as calls. To see which channel brings the booked calls, follow my tutorial on {{< a key="utm-ga4-funnel-tracking" >}}tracking a funnel with UTM links and GA4{{< /a >}}.

Here is the maths for the accountant above, with invented round numbers (an illustration, not a benchmark):

| Stage | One month | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Visits to service pages | 500 | Starting point |
| Enquiries | 20 | 4% of visits |
| Conversations | 14 | 70% of enquiries |
| Quotes sent | 10 | About 70% of conversations |
| New clients | 4 | 40% of quotes |

Now look for a fifth client. Option one: 25% more visitors, which takes months of SEO or an ads budget. Option two: speak to 18 of the 20 enquirers instead of 14, by replying faster; with the same rates further down, that gives about 13 quotes and 5 clients. The second option costs a phone alert and a habit. **Fix the bottom of the funnel before you pour more people in at the top.**

## Where funnels leak, and how to plug each leak

Leaks on the page itself show up when you watch real visits: I use Microsoft Clarity to see where visitors engage and what makes them leave, and my guide to {{< a key="website-conversion-rate" >}}why a website gets no enquiries{{< /a >}} explains the method. The five leaks below happen once the visitor is ready to contact you, so each one loses a prospect you had already won over.

### Slow follow-up

The best-known study on reply speed is old but clear. In "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads", published in Harvard Business Review in 2011, researchers analysed 1.25 million leads received by 42 US companies: firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it (in the study, to have a real conversation with a decision-maker) as firms that tried an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or more ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads)). In an audit reported in the same article, only 37% of the companies responded within an hour, and 23% never responded at all.

Speed is also a volume problem. Pilotech now gets about ten prospects a day from SEO and cold emailing together, and at that rate memory and a notebook stop working. The founder asked me to build an app that shows every prospect in one place, so that nobody is forgotten or left waiting for a call back. You do not need a custom app to start: {{< a key="automate-lead-follow-up" >}}instant alerts and call-back reminders{{< /a >}} can be set up for free.

### No clear next step

A page that ends on a paragraph about your values leaves visitors to work out what to do next. End every service page with the same one or two actions, written as what will happen: "Call and you speak to an accountant, not a switchboard", "Book a 15-minute call". One main action per page beats five buttons.

### Long forms

Each extra field is one more reason to give up on a phone screen. Ask only what you need to reply: a name, a phone number or email address, the service, and the town if you travel. Budget, timing and the rest can wait for the call.

### No booking link

Some people will not call, and nobody enjoys three emails to find a slot. A booking page lets them pick a time at 11 pm and turns an enquiry into a booked call in one step. I prefer YouCanBookMe: it has the features of Calendly, a smaller team, it is easy to find your way around and it costs a little less, though Calendly is good too. YouCanBookMe's free plan covers one booking page, and a personal Google account can create one booking page with Google Calendar's [appointment schedules](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/10729749).

### Nobody answering the phone

For local services, the phone is often the main entrance, and a call that rings out sends the prospect to the next result. If you spend the day on site or with clients, record a voicemail greeting that says when you will call back, keep that promise, and block two short call-back slots in your day. A missed call is an enquiry: count it.

## What not to copy from e-commerce funnels

Online shops and course sellers build funnels for a click-to-buy sale. Some of their favourite tools travel badly to a service business.

- **Tripwires.** A tripwire is a cheap first product, such as a short guide or a trial for a few pounds, meant to turn a subscriber into a buyer. For a service, the first purchase is usually the service itself, and a free call does that job better. For people who are not ready to talk yet, give away something useful instead: see my {{< a key="lead-magnet-ideas" >}}lead magnet ideas for service businesses{{< /a >}}.
- **Countdown timers and fake scarcity.** A timer that restarts on every visit tells a careful buyer you cannot be trusted. It can also break the law: when you sell to consumers, falsely claiming that an offer is available only for a very limited time is banned in all circumstances, under point 7 of Annex I of the [EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32005L0029) and paragraph 7 of [Schedule 20 to the UK's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20/paragraph/7), in force since 6 April 2025. A real deadline, stated honestly, is fine.
- **Long email sequences before any human contact.** Automated sequences suit low-priced products that people buy without talking to anyone. For a service worth hundreds or thousands, the conversation is the demonstration, and every email that delays it lets the prospect go cold. Use email to get people to the call, and to follow up after it.

This is a summary, not legal advice: if you plan a campaign around a deadline or a limited offer, have a lawyer check the wording.

## Next steps: map your own funnel this week

1. List your entrances: Google, your Google Business Profile, referrals, cold emails, social media. Note where your last ten clients first heard of you.
2. Test your own funnel from your phone: fill in your form, call your number outside office hours and try to book a call. Time the reply.
3. Fill in last month's numbers, even roughly: visits, enquiries, conversations, quotes, clients.
4. Fix the leak nearest the bottom first: reply time, missed calls, quotes nobody chased. Then the page, the form and the booking link. Traffic comes last.
5. Build the rest with the tools you already have, as shown in my tutorial on {{< a key="build-sales-funnel" >}}building a funnel with your website, a booking page and email{{< /a >}}.

If the test shows that the website itself is the leak (slow pages, no phone button on mobile, a form that lands in spam), fix the site before you pay for any funnel builder.
