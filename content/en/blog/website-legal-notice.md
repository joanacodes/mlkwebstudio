A website legal notice is the page, or the block in your footer, that says who runs the site and how to reach them. The website legal notice requirements are close in the EU and the UK, because both come from the same EU directive of 2000. France asks for the most: a phone number, the share capital, a named publication director and the details of the company that hosts the site, on a page called the *mentions légales*.

This guide lists what to publish in each country, for a company and for a sole trader, with a template at the end. I build websites for small teams in France and the UK, and the legal pages are part of every site I deliver. The guide belongs to my {{< a key="gdpr-guide" >}}GDPR guide for small teams{{< /a >}}, next to the privacy policy and the cookie banner.

It sets out the rules as they stand on 19 October 2026, and it is general information, not legal advice. The good news: a legal notice is a list of facts you already have, and you rarely need a lawyer to write one.

## What a website legal notice is, and what it is not

A legal notice answers one question: who is behind this website? It names the business, says where it is established and gives a way to contact it. Careful clients read it before paying a deposit, and so do suppliers, journalists and anyone with a complaint.

It is often confused with the other pages in the footer, which answer different questions:

| Page | The question it answers | Main rules |
|---|---|---|
| Legal notice | Who runs this site, and how do I reach them? | E-Commerce Directive, Article 5; the LCEN in France; the E-Commerce Regulations 2002 and the company disclosure rules in the UK |
| Privacy policy | What personal data do you collect, why, for how long, and what are my rights? | GDPR and UK GDPR, Articles 13 and 14 |
| Cookie information | Which trackers do you use, and how do I refuse them? | ePrivacy Directive, Article 5(3); PECR in the UK |
| Terms of sale | On what terms do I buy from you? | Consumer law: the Code de la consommation in France, the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 in the UK |

The privacy policy has its own guide: {{< a key="privacy-policy-guide" >}}what a privacy policy must say{{< /a >}}. The cookie rules, which differ more between France and the UK, are in {{< a key="cookie-banner-rules" >}}my guide to cookie banner requirements{{< /a >}}.

## The EU baseline: Article 5 of the E-Commerce Directive

Every EU country applies the same minimum, set by [Article 5 of Directive 2000/31/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/31/oj) on electronic commerce. A business website must make the following “easily, directly and permanently accessible”:

- your name: the legal name of the business, not only a brand;
- the geographic address where you are established;
- your contact details, including an email address, so people can reach you quickly and directly;
- the trade register you are entered in and your registration number;
- the supervisory authority, if your activity needs an authorisation;
- for a regulated profession, such as architect, accountant or lawyer: your professional body, your professional title and the country that granted it, and a reference to the professional rules that apply to you;
- your VAT identification number, if your activity is subject to VAT.

Article 5 also requires prices, where you show them, to say clearly whether they include tax and delivery costs. The Digital Services Act has since replaced the directive’s rules on the liability of online intermediaries, but Article 5 still applies.

Each country writes this list into its own law, and an EU business follows the rules of the country where it is established (Article 3). That is why a German site has an *Impressum* and a French one has *mentions légales*: the names differ, the core is the same.

## Website legal notice requirements in France: the mentions légales

France has the longest list, and it applies to every professional website, whatever the size of the business. It comes from the [loi pour la confiance dans l’économie numérique](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/JORFTEXT000000801164) of 21 June 2004 (LCEN). The core list now sits in its article 1-1, where the SREN law of 21 May 2024 moved it from the former article 6, III. The LCEN also transposes the directive’s Article 5, which is where the email address, the VAT number and the regulated-profession details come from.

| What to publish | Company (SARL, SAS…) | Sole trader (EI, micro-entrepreneur) |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Legal name and legal form | Your surname and first name, with “EI” or “entrepreneur individuel”, as on your invoices |
| Address | Registered office | Your address |
| Phone and email | Yes | Yes |
| Registration number | SIREN number, written “RCS” plus the registry’s town and the number for a commercial company | SIREN number |
| Share capital | Yes | Not applicable |
| VAT number | If you have one | If you have one |
| Publication director | The legal representative, such as the gérant or the président | You |
| Host | Legal name, address and phone number of the company that hosts the site | The same |

Regulated professions add their professional body, their title and the rules they follow: an architect, for example, names the regional council of the Ordre des architectes they are registered with.

The penalty is criminal: up to one year in prison and a €75,000 fine for an individual or a company’s manager, and up to €375,000 for a company. Convictions are rare, and the everyday cost is quieter. I want the sites I build to make a business look serious enough to be taken at its price, and a missing or half-empty legal notice works against that with any client who checks.

### If you sell to consumers: terms of sale and a mediator

A site that sells to consumers, such as an online shop or a booking site, adds terms of sale (*conditions générales de vente*, or CGV) and a consumer mediator. Since 1 January 2016, every professional must let consumers use a consumer mediator free of charge (article L612-1 of the Code de la consommation) and show the mediator’s contact details and website visibly, for example on its website and in its terms of sale (articles L616-1 and R616-1). The administrative fine for leaving them out can reach €3,000 for an individual and €15,000 for a company (article L641-1). A site that only sells to businesses is not concerned.

{{< callout type="warning" >}}If your terms or your legal notice still link to the EU’s online dispute resolution (ODR) platform, remove the link. **The platform closed on 20 July 2025**, when Regulation (EU) 2024/3228 repealed the regulation that required online traders to link to it. The duty to name a consumer mediator in France has not changed.{{< /callout >}}

## What a UK website must display

UK law has no page called a legal notice, but the information is compulsory all the same, and it comes from two places.

1. **The Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002**, still in force after Brexit. [Regulation 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/6) follows Article 5 of the directive: your name, a geographic address, an email address, your register and registration number, your VAT number if you are VAT-registered, plus the supervisory authority and regulated-profession details where they apply. It covers sole traders and partnerships as well as companies.
2. **The trading disclosure rules** for companies, in the Company, Limited Liability Partnership and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015. A limited company’s website must show its registered name, its company number, its registered office address and the part of the UK where it is registered (England and Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland), as [GOV.UK sums up](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material).

Many UK companies cover both with a few lines at the bottom of every page:

```
[Company name] Ltd · Registered in England and Wales · Company number [number]
Registered office: [full address] · VAT number: GB[9 digits]
Contact: [email address]
```

A sole trader has no company number to show. The name the 2002 Regulations ask for is the name of the person who runs the business, so put your own name next to your trading name, with a geographic address and an email address.

Failing to make the company disclosures is an offence committed by the company and by every officer in default, punishable by a fine of up to £1,000. Section 83 of the Companies Act 2006 adds a civil risk: if the company sues on a contract made while it was in breach, the court can dismiss the claim when the other side shows that the missing details cost it money or stopped it from bringing its own claim.

## Selling into France or the EU from outside it

The rules above apply to businesses established in each country. If you sell from London, Geneva, Montreal or New York to customers in France, two other sets of rules reach you.

- **Consumer law.** When you direct your sales at consumers in an EU country, the consumer protections of their country generally apply to the contract (Article 6 of the Rome I Regulation). They include the duty to give your identity, geographic address, phone number and email address before people order. In the other direction, an EU business selling to consumers in the UK meets a similar list in the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013.
- **The GDPR.** If you target people in the EU, your privacy policy must say who you are, and you may need a representative in the EU. My guide on {{< a key="gdpr-outside-eu" >}}whether the GDPR applies outside the EU{{< /a >}} sets out the two tests.

The simplest answer is one legal page that meets the longest list, the French one, plus the UK lines if your company is registered in the UK. A UK company selling to French clients then has a page they recognise.

## Where to put it, and the mistakes to avoid

Link it from the footer of every page, with a plain label: “Legal notice”, or “Mentions légales” on a French site. One click from any page is the usual way to make it easily, directly and permanently accessible. Write it as text on a web page, not as an image, so that visitors, screen readers, search engines and AI assistants can all read it.

The mistakes worth checking for:

- **Naming the registrar as the host.** The host is the company whose servers store the site, such as Squarespace, Wix, Shopify, OVHcloud or o2switch. The registrar is where you bought the domain name, and it is often a different company: my guide to {{< a key="domain-hosting-email-guide" >}}domains and hosting{{< /a >}} explains the difference.
- **Not updating it after a move.** Many of my clients have moved from WordPress to Squarespace, and each move changes the host. A new registered office or a capital increase changes the company lines.
- **Showing a brand only.** A trading name is not enough: the notice needs the legal name behind it, the company’s or the person’s.
- **A copied template** with someone else’s details still in it, or placeholders such as [VAT number] left in place.
- **One page that mixes the legal notice and the privacy policy** until neither is complete.

## Your legal notice in five steps, with a template

1. Get your official record: the Companies House entry for a UK company, your Kbis or RNE extract in France, and your VAT number if you have one.
2. Find your host’s legal name, address and phone number in its own legal pages.
3. Fill in the template below, deleting the lines that do not apply to you.
4. Publish it as a page and link it from the footer of every page, next to your privacy policy and cookie settings.
5. Check it once a year, and after any move, change of platform or change in the company.

```
Legal notice

This website is published by [legal name and legal form].
UK company: registered in [England and Wales], company number [number]
French company: share capital [amount] · RCS [town] [SIREN number]
Registered office: [full address]
Phone: [number] · Email: [address]
VAT number: [number, if you have one]
Publication director (France): [name, role]
Host (France): [host's legal name, address, phone number]
Regulated profession: [professional body, title, country, rules]
Consumer mediator (France, sales to consumers): [name, website]
```

You do not need a lawyer for the legal notice itself. One earns their fee on the documents around it: terms of sale for an online shop, a marketplace where other people sell, a regulated profession, or a site that publishes content from its users.
