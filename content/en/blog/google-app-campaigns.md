Google App campaigns are Google Ads campaigns built to promote a mobile app. You give Google a few lines of text, some images and videos, and its systems assemble the ads and show them on Google Search, YouTube, Discover, the websites and apps that carry Google ads and, for Android apps, Google Play. You do not choose keywords or placements: you choose a goal, what you are willing to pay for it, a budget, the countries and the languages.

That makes them quick to launch, and just as quick to waste money on. My view of Google Ads is that the tool is not that tricky, but you need to research your target and what they search for before you spend anything. With App campaigns, that research goes into three things Google cannot do for you: the assets, the tracking and the numbers behind your budget.

This guide explains how App campaigns work as of October 2026 and how to work out a first budget you can defend. Where paid ads fit among your other launch channels is covered in my {{< a key="mobile-app-launch-guide" >}}guide to launching a mobile app{{< /a >}}.

## How Google App campaigns work

App campaigns used to be called Universal App Campaigns (UAC), a name you will still meet in older guides. The principle has not changed: one campaign, several Google properties, and ads built automatically from your assets. Google's help page [About App campaigns](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6247380) lists where they can appear:

- **Google Search**, among the results;
- **Google Play**, for Android apps only;
- **YouTube**, where your videos do most of the work;
- **Discover**, the feed of articles in the Google app;
- **the Google Display Network**, the websites and apps that show Google ads, including apps that run ads through AdMob.

An iOS app can be advertised too, but Google Play only lists Android apps, so its ads run on Google's other properties. App campaigns never place your app inside the App Store: that is Apple's own ad system, covered in my {{< a key="apple-ads-tutorial" >}}Apple Ads tutorial{{< /a >}}.

Google mixes your text, images and videos into ads of different formats, tests the combinations and shows the best performers more often. Install ads lead to your store listing, so a weak listing wastes every click you pay for. My guide to {{< a key="app-store-listing" >}}App Store and Google Play listings{{< /a >}} covers the title, the screenshots and the description that do the rest of the selling.

## The three types of App campaign

When you create the campaign, you pick one of three subtypes:

| Subtype | What it is for | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| App installs | Winning new users | An app published on Google Play or the App Store |
| App engagement | Bringing existing users back to do something in the app | Google's help asks for at least 50,000 installs |
| App pre-registration | Collecting sign-ups on Google Play before the release | Android only |

For a new app, the choice is made for you: installs, or pre-registration if your Android app is not out yet. Engagement campaigns come later, once enough people have the app for bringing them back to be worth paying for.

Install campaigns also accept optional audience signals: hints about the kind of people most likely to install your app, which Google uses to guide its search for them.

## The assets you give Google

You write the text and supply the visuals; Google handles the layout. The limits per ad group, from Google's specifications as of October 2026:

- headlines: up to 5, 30 characters each;
- descriptions: up to 5, 90 characters each;
- images: up to 20, in horizontal (1.91:1), vertical (4:5) and square (1:1) formats;
- videos: up to 20, uploaded to YouTube first;
- HTML5 ads: optional, and most small teams can skip them.

Google advises filling the maximum, and it makes sense: each placement has its own formats, and the more material Google has, the more combinations it can test. A screen recording of the app in use, with captions so it works without sound, makes a fair first video.

Write each headline so it makes sense on its own, because Google mixes them. When I write copy, I start with what people complain about most, and the reviews of the apps your users already have are full of it. If reviewers of a rival meal-planning app say that planning takes forever, a headline such as "Plan your week in 5 min" answers them directly.

After launch, the asset report rates each asset once it leaves the Learning status: Low, Good or Best. Google's advice: when you have reached the maximum, replace the Low assets, and add new ones in the style of the Best.

## Bidding: choose what you pay for

There are no keyword bids in an App campaign. You tell Google what to optimise for and, usually, how much you want to pay for it on average:

| Goal | Bid you set | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Install volume | Target cost per install (target CPI) | A new app that needs its first users |
| In-app actions | Target cost per action (target CPA), such as a sign-up or a purchase | Once that action is tracked reliably |
| In-app action value | Target return on ad spend (target ROAS) | Apps that sell inside the app, with steady purchase data |

Google has also added goals without a target, where it simply gets as many installs, or as much value, as the budget allows. They are easier to set up, but the budget then becomes your only brake. Either way, a target is an average, not a cap: some installs will cost more than the target and some less.

An install that is never opened is a cost, not a user. So as soon as you can track a meaningful first action, a completed sign-up or a first purchase, consider moving to in-app actions, which teaches Google to look for the people who take it. For target ROAS, Google's help asks for at least 10 conversions a day (300 over 30 days) and suggests running a target CPA campaign first, to know the return you start from.

### Setting your target cost per install

Work backwards from what a new user brings you. Say one install in 20 becomes a subscriber at £4.99 a month who stays four months on average: each install brings in about £1, before the store's commission and VAT. Your target CPI has to sit well below that pound, or every install loses money.

## How much to budget for a Google App campaign

Google's [best practices for App campaigns](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6167162) give the rule: when you optimise for install volume, set a daily budget of at least 50 times your target CPI. For in-app actions, the floor is 10 times your target CPA (another Google page says 10 to 15 times). The reason is data: the system needs enough conversions every day to learn who converts.

Here is what that means in money. The monthly column uses Google's general budget rule: a campaign can spend up to twice its daily budget on a given day, but you are not charged more than 30.4 times the daily budget in a month.

| Your target | Minimum daily budget | About per month |
|---|---|---|
| £0.50 per install | £25 | £760 |
| £1 per install | £50 | £1,520 |
| £2 per install | £100 | £3,040 |
| £8 per sign-up (target CPA) | £80 | £2,432 |

Two extras reach the invoice: Google adds a 2% fee on ads served in the UK (and another 2% on ads served in France), and VAT is billed separately.

The rule also works the other way round: divide what you can spend per day by 50 to find the highest target CPI your budget supports. With £20 a day, that is £0.40 per install. If installs in your category cost more than that, the campaign is likely to struggle to spend its budget and to gather enough installs to learn.

In that case, be honest with yourself: the money may work harder elsewhere first. My comparison of {{< a key="app-ads-budget-comparison" >}}where to spend your app's first £1,000{{< /a >}} weighs Google against Apple Ads and Meta, and my article on {{< a key="seo-vs-ppc" >}}SEO vs PPC{{< /a >}} explains when paying for each visit beats investing in rankings. Whatever you choose, treat the first month as a test you can afford to lose.

## Tracking installs and in-app actions

App campaigns optimise on the conversions you send back to Google Ads. Without tracking, Google bids blind, so set it up and test it before the campaign goes live. There are three ways to feed Google Ads:

1. **Firebase** (Google Analytics for Firebase), Google's own SDK, on Android and iOS. Your developer adds it to the app, and the events that matter, a sign-up or a purchase, are imported into Google Ads as conversions.
2. **Google Play**, for Android apps: Google Ads can measure installs through Google Play. Do not expect its totals to match the Play Console, because Google Ads only counts installs from its ads, while Google Play also counts direct ones.
3. **A third-party attribution partner**, also called a mobile measurement partner, such as AppsFlyer or Adjust: useful when you advertise on several networks and want one source of truth.

Even if you start with installs, have your developer send your key in-app events from day one, so the data is already there when you move to in-app actions.

### iOS: less data, more modelling

On iPhone, Apple's App Tracking Transparency (ATT) rules oblige an app to ask permission before tracking people across other companies' apps and websites, and people can say no. Google's [iOS best practices](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10384955) come down to four points:

- use on-device conversion measurement through Firebase: the matching happens on the phone, and no information that identifies the user leaves it;
- let the Firebase SDK handle SKAdNetwork, Apple's attribution framework, and set up a conversion value schema that covers the events you optimise for;
- decide whether the ATT prompt makes sense for your app: people who accept it give Google more observed conversions to learn from;
- keep to eight or fewer iOS install campaigns per app, as Google says more harms performance and SKAdNetwork reporting.

Expect iOS reports to rely more on modelled conversions, and judge iOS campaigns on trends over several weeks rather than day by day.

### Consent for users in Europe, the UK and Switzerland

If people in the European Economic Area, the UK or Switzerland use your app, Google's EU user consent policy requires you to collect their consent for the data your app shares with Google for advertising. That applies wherever your company is based: a UK app with users in France is concerned, and so is a Canadian app with users in Germany. In an app, it means a consent screen, then passing each person's choice to the Firebase SDK, which Google calls [consent mode for apps](https://developers.google.com/tag-platform/security/guides/app-consent).

My guide to {{< a key="app-privacy-labels" >}}GDPR consent and app privacy labels{{< /a >}} covers the screen itself and what to declare in the stores. This is general information, not legal advice: if your app handles health data, children's data or financial details, an hour with a privacy lawyer or a DPO is worth it.

## The first weeks: learning period and common mistakes

A new campaign goes through a learning period while Google works out who installs. Google advises allowing 7 to 14 days for the system to stabilise before you judge it, and avoiding changes of more than 20% to the budget or the target at a time. Big swings also make the results impossible to read.

After that, read each campaign: installs, cost per install, the cost of the in-app action you care about, and the asset report. Compare the cost per install with the target you worked out from what a user is worth, not with what feels cheap.

The mistakes I would watch for:

- **A daily budget below 50 times the target.** The campaign cannot gather enough conversions to learn, and the test proves nothing.
- **Tracking added after launch.** Google optimises on what it receives; a week without events is a week of learning thrown away.
- **Staying on installs too long.** Installs are the cheapest conversion and the least telling one.
- **Too few assets.** One headline and one image give Google nothing to test.
- **Locations and languages that do not match the assets.** Google shows the ads in the locations and languages you choose, and an English-only listing advertised in France is unlikely to convert well.
- **Changing everything every morning.** Keep to the 20% rule.

## When to start, and a launch checklist

App campaigns are worth testing when your store listing already turns visitors into installs, your tracking works and the budget the 50x rule gives is money you can risk for a month. If one of those is missing, fix it first: the campaign multiplies what is already there, good or bad.

I give the same warning about every new channel: it is easy to chase the next shiny tool, and it pays more to get the most from the channels that already bring you users before adding one you will not have time to watch. You do not need an agency for a first App campaign either. If you also run Search ads for your website, my guide to {{< a key="google-ads-small-business" >}}the basics of Google Ads{{< /a >}} covers the research to do first.

Before you launch, check that you have:

- an app live on Google Play or the App Store, or an Android app ready for pre-registration;
- a store listing that converts;
- Firebase, Google Play or an attribution partner sending installs and at least one in-app event to Google Ads, tested;
- a consent screen for users in the EEA, the UK and Switzerland;
- five headlines, five descriptions, images in the three formats and at least one video;
- a target CPI worked out from what a user is worth, and a daily budget of at least 50 times that target;
- locations and languages that match your listing and your assets;
- a date in your diary two weeks after launch, and no big changes before it.
