Someone has just given you their email address. For a few days they remember who you are and why they signed up; three weeks from now, your first newsletter may look like a message from a stranger. A welcome email sequence puts those first days to use: it is a short series of automated emails that every new subscriber receives, in the same order, from the moment they join your list.

Below are the five emails I would send for a service business, each with its timing, its purpose and a short template to adapt, followed by the set-up in your email tool and what to measure.

## Why the first days matter

Welcome emails are among the most clicked emails a business sends. In [GetResponse's 2024 email marketing benchmarks](https://www.getresponse.com/resources/reports/email-marketing-benchmarks), welcome emails had an average click-through rate of 16.6%, against 3.84% for newsletters: more than four times as many clicks. It is an average across one vendor's customers, so take the ratio from it, not a target.

The reason is simple: the person asked for this email a few minutes ago. A sequence spreads your introduction over ten days, so each email has one job and stays short.

## The welcome email sequence at a glance

| Email | When | Its job | What it asks for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Straight away | Deliver what you promised, say what comes next | A reply, if they have a question |
| 2 | Day 2 | Explain why you do this work | A one-line reply |
| 3 | Day 4 | Share your most useful guide | A click |
| 4 | Day 7 | Show real proof | A click |
| 5 | Day 10 | Make a soft offer, with an easy way out | A call, or nothing |

Days count from the sign-up, or from the confirmation click if you use double opt-in. The gaps are short at the start, while interest is high, and wider after.

## Who should get the sequence, and who should not

The sequence is for people who asked for your emails, through a newsletter sign-up form or a separate, unticked box under your lead magnet form. Someone who only downloaded a checklist gets the email that delivers it, and nothing else. My article on {{< a key="lead-magnet-ideas" >}}lead magnets for service businesses{{< /a >}} shows how to keep the two apart.

There are exceptions, such as the soft opt-in for existing customers and looser rules for some business addresses. The rules also follow your subscribers: a UK, Swiss or US business whose newsletter targets people in the EU falls under the EU GDPR for them (Article 3(2)). My guide to {{< a key="newsletter-consent-gdpr" >}}newsletter consent{{< /a >}} explains the details. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Email 1, straight away: deliver what you promised

The first email has two jobs: hand over what the person signed up for, and tell them what is coming. Send it within minutes. Link to the resource rather than attaching it, so you can correct the file without sending anything again, and invite questions: a reply is the surest sign that your email arrived and was read.

```text
Subject: Your [resource] is here

Hi [first name],

Thank you for signing up. Here is your [resource]: [link]

Over the next ten days I will send you four short emails: why I do this work, the guide my clients find most useful, a real project, and how to reach me. After that, the newsletter arrives once a month.

Any questions? Just reply: this address reaches me.

[Your name]
[Business name], [phone number]
```

## Email 2, day 2: why you do this work

People hire someone they trust, and trust starts with knowing why and how you work. Day 2 is for one short story and one promise, not your CV.

For my studio, the story would end with the sentence I use with friends: I help businesses be seen, bring prospects in, feel proud of their online presence and automate their workflow to make their lives easier. The promise would be the honest advice my clients already get: I tell them when they should call someone else rather than me.

```text
Subject: Why I do this

Hi [first name],

[Two or three sentences: how you started, or the problem you kept seeing.]

What I do now, in one sentence: [your version].

And one promise: if you ask me something and I am not the right person, I will tell you who is.

[Your name]

P.S. What made you sign up? Reply in one line: I read every answer.
```

## Email 3, day 4: your most useful guide

Email 3 gives before it asks: the single most useful thing you have written. To choose it, start from the questions prospects ask on a first call. Mine ask whether I have experience, what SEO will do for them and how long it takes to rank; any one of those answers would make a good third email.

Put the short answer in the email, for readers who will not click, and link to the full guide on your website.

```text
Subject: The question I hear most

Hi [first name],

The question I hear most on first calls is: "[question]"

The short answer: [two or three sentences].

The full answer, with examples, is here: [link to your guide]

[Your name]
```

## Email 4, day 7: proof, and only real proof

By day 7 the reader knows what you do; now show that it works. Tell one real project in three lines, the problem, what you did and what changed, then link to the full case study.

Only real proof: a client you name with their permission, figures you can show, photos of your own work. If you have no case study yet, describe a recent job honestly and leave the numbers out rather than rounding them up. For my studio, it would be Pilotech, an air conditioning installer in Paris and the Île-de-France: a website built in about a month, and a first lead three weeks after it went live, with a link to my {{< a page="/projects" >}}project pages{{< /a >}}.

```text
Subject: What changed for [client or type of client]

Hi [first name],

[Client] came to me with [problem].
We [what you did, in one line].
[What changed, with a date or a figure you can show.]

The whole story, with photos: [link to the case study]

[Your name]
```

## Email 5, day 10: a soft offer and an easy way out

The last email makes the offer: a short call, a visit or a quote, whatever your first step is. Soft means one invitation, no countdown and no "last chance" in the subject line.

Give a phone number next to the booking link. In my cold email campaigns, many prospects call rather than reply: a call is quicker than writing an email, and people like talking to a human. For the booking page, my preference is YouCanBookMe, but Calendly does the job too.

End with a line on preferences or unsubscribing. Your email tool adds an unsubscribe link to every message, as the law expects every marketing email to offer a way to opt out, and [Google's email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126) also ask senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail addresses for one-click unsubscribe and a visible link in the body. Saying it in words, in the last email, shows that you mean it.

```text
Subject: Shall we talk about [problem]?

Hi [first name],

This is the last email of the welcome series. From now on, the newsletter arrives once a month.

If you would rather talk about [problem] now, book a 15-minute call: [booking link]
Or call me on [phone number].

Not for you? No problem: choose what you receive, or unsubscribe, here: [preferences link]

[Your name]
```

## Plain text or a designed template

Plain text, or a light template that looks like a personal email (one column, your name, no banner), often feels more personal, which suits a sequence meant to start a conversation.

In cold emails I go further, with plain text, a phone number and no links at all, as I explain in {{< a key="plain-text-cold-emails" >}}why I never put links in cold emails{{< /a >}}. A welcome sequence is different: the person asked for it, and most of these emails need a link. Nor does a newsletter have to be plain: if your work is visual, as an interior designer's is, a designed template with real photos makes sense.

## How to set up the sequence in your email tool

You do not need a funnel builder: the tool that sends your newsletter can run the sequence. It is easy to buy a new tool for every idea; the one you already have usually does the job.

1. Create an automation (some tools call it a workflow, a journey or a sequence) that starts when someone joins the list or group your sign-up form feeds. With double opt-in, check that it starts after the confirmation click.
2. Add email 1 with no delay, then waits of 2, 2, 3 and 3 days before emails 2 to 5.
3. Send as a person, from an address on your own domain that receives replies, never from a no-reply address.
4. Give the first name a fallback, such as "Hi there", for sign-ups that left it empty.
5. If your tool allows it, add an exit condition: someone who books a call or becomes a client leaves the sequence.
6. After email 5, move subscribers to your regular newsletter; my {{< a key="b2b-newsletter-ideas" >}}B2B newsletter ideas{{< /a >}} help with what to send next.

Check where the free plan stops before you build. At the time of writing (October 2026), Kit's free plan has no sequences or visual automations, while MailerLite's includes three automations, enough for this sequence. ## What to measure: clicks and replies, not opens

Open rates look precise, but they are not. An open is counted when a tiny image in the email loads, and [Apple's Mail Privacy Protection](https://www.apple.com/uk/legal/privacy/data/en/mail-privacy-protection/) downloads that content in the background whether or not the email is opened: an open can be recorded for an email nobody read.

If you have subscribers in France, there is a second reason. The CNIL, France's data protection regulator, published a [recommendation on email tracking pixels](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-04/recommandation-pixels_de_suivi.pdf) in April 2026: measuring opens for marketing purposes needs the reader's consent, separate from the consent to receive your emails. It does not deal with tracked links, but the CNIL ties consent to the purpose rather than the technology, so check its guidance before you profile readers by their clicks too (as of 19 October 2026).

Watch these instead, email by email:

- **clicks** on the one link that matters;
- **replies**, which need no tracking at all;
- **booked calls** from email 5, with UTM parameters on the booking link;
- **unsubscribes**: a jump after one email tells you which one to rewrite.

Wait until 50 to 100 people have been through the sequence before you judge it, then change one email at a time. That is how I improve cold email copy: tests run over time show which version gets responses, and that version stays. ## Checklist before you switch it on

1. The sign-up form says what people will receive, and how often.
2. Only people who asked for your emails enter the sequence.
3. Email 1 arrives within minutes, and its link works.
4. Each email has one job and one main link.
5. Email 4 uses real proof, shared with the client's permission.
6. The sender is a person on your domain, and replies reach you.
7. Every email carries an unsubscribe link.
8. You tested the sign-up (your own address, plus a Gmail and an Outlook one if you can) and read every email on a phone.

You do not need me for any of this: an afternoon is enough to write and set up five emails. It gets more involved when each sign-up must also reach a CRM, a lead list or a booking tool, and that is where an automation earns its keep.
