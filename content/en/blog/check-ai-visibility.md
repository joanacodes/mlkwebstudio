AI visibility is how often AI assistants name your business when someone asks them about what you sell. If a customer asks ChatGPT for a reliable office cleaning company in Manchester and your company is in the answer, you are visible. If three competitors are named instead, you have work to do.

There is no ranking to look up, so the only way to know is to ask the assistants yourself, the way your customers do. This tutorial gives you a free method for any small business: ten prompts, a spreadsheet and an hour or so each month. Then come the free reports from Microsoft and Google, the paid trackers, and what to change when the results disappoint.

It belongs to my {{< a key="ai-search-geo-guide" >}}guide to generative engine optimisation{{< /a >}}, which explains how assistants choose the businesses they mention. For the test itself, a browser and a spreadsheet are enough.

## What AI visibility means for a small business

An assistant can treat your business in four ways, and your spreadsheet will record each of them:

- **Named:** your business appears in the answer.
- **Linked:** the answer links to your website, not only to a directory that lists you.
- **Recommended:** you are named first, or presented as a good fit for the question.
- **Described correctly:** your services, area, prices and contact details are right.

Being named with the wrong details can be worse than not being named at all: a customer may call about a service you stopped offering last year.

AI visibility is also different from AI traffic. Plenty of people read a name in an answer, then call or search for the business without clicking anything. In my experience, people would rather call than write because it is quicker, so a business can be visible in ChatGPT while its analytics show almost nothing. My tutorial on how to {{< a key="track-ai-traffic" >}}track AI traffic in GA4{{< /a >}} covers the visits; this one covers the mentions.

## Step 1: write ten prompts your customers would type

A prompt is the question someone types into an assistant. People write longer, more conversational prompts than Google searches, and they often describe their situation. Take an office cleaning company in Manchester:

| Type of prompt | Example |
|---|---|
| Service and town | "Office cleaning company in Manchester city centre" |
| Best for a need | "Best office cleaning company for a small accountancy firm in Manchester" |
| Near a place, with a deadline | "Office cleaners near Salford Quays who can start next week" |
| A problem to solve | "Our office carpets are stained and a client visits on Friday, who can deep clean them in Manchester?" |
| Comparison | "[Your business] or [competitor]: which is better for a 20-person office?" |
| Alternatives | "Alternatives to [competitor] for office cleaning in Manchester" |
| Price | "How much does office cleaning cost in Manchester?" |
| Your name | "What do you know about [your business] in Manchester?" |
| Reputation | "[Your business] reviews", "Is [your business] reliable?" |

Ten prompts are enough to start. More prompts give you more rows to fill, not necessarily more insight.

To find the words your customers use, look in four places:

- **Your phone calls.** Questions my clients ask on a first call, such as "Do you have experience?" or "How long will it take?", are what people now ask an assistant before calling anyone.
- **Search Console.** The searches that already show your site on Google reveal the words people use for your trade.
- **Keyword costs.** I use Semrush mainly for keyword research and for what keywords cost in ads. Advertisers pay most for the searches that bring business, so an expensive keyword usually makes a good prompt.
- **Deep research.** I use deep research tools to find what people in a niche complain about most and which words keep coming back. My method is in {{< a key="deep-research-copywriting-seo" >}}how to use Deep Research for copywriting and SEO{{< /a >}}.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}**Freeze the wording.** Keep your prompts in the spreadsheet and paste them every month, word for word. Change one word and you are comparing two different tests.{{< /callout >}}

## Step 2: set up your spreadsheet

Create a spreadsheet in Google Sheets or Excel, with one tab per month and these columns:

| Column | Header | What to enter |
|---|---|---|
| A | Date | The day of the test |
| B | Assistant | ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or Google |
| C | Prompt | The exact wording from your list |
| D | Named | Yes or No |
| E | Position | 1 if you are named first, 2 if second, and so on |
| F | Link | Yes if the answer links to your website |
| G | What it said | The sentence about you, copied word for word |
| H | Accurate | Yes, No or Partly |
| I | Competitors | The other businesses named, in order |
| J | Sources | The sites the answer cites, or "none" |

Each answer is one row: ten prompts in four assistants make forty rows a month.

## Step 3: ask each assistant without memory or history

Your own account would skew the test: an assistant that remembers your chats, or knows you run the business, may mention you more readily than it would for a stranger. Test as a new customer would, logged out where you can, otherwise in a chat without memory, and start a new chat for every prompt so one answer does not colour the next.

### ChatGPT

Open chatgpt.com in a private window and do not sign in. ChatGPT search has been open to logged-out users since February 2025, so a logged-out chat can still search the web, as it would for a new visitor.

An answer without sources most likely comes from what the model learned in training, not from a search. Write "none" in the Sources column: new pages and mentions can change a search answer fairly quickly, while training data only changes with a new model.

{{< callout type="warning" >}}**A Temporary Chat is not automatically neutral.** If you prefer to stay signed in, OpenAI's help centre says a temporary chat can still use your memories and custom instructions unless you switch personalisation off before the conversation starts. A logged-out private window is simpler.{{< /callout >}}

{{< screenshot id="check-ai-visibility-02" alt="ChatGPT in a private window, logged out, answering a local service prompt with businesses named and sources listed" >}}

### Gemini

In Gemini, if you use it with a Google account, start a Temporary Chat. Google says these chats are not used to personalise Gemini or to train its models, and they are kept for up to 72 hours. Gemini's memory of past chats is switched on by default, one more reason not to test in an ordinary chat.

### Perplexity

Perplexity works without an account, so a private window is enough. It numbers its sources and lists them with each answer, which makes the Sources column quick to fill.

{{< screenshot id="check-ai-visibility-03" alt="Perplexity answer to a local service prompt with its numbered sources" >}}

### Google AI Overviews and AI Mode

Search each prompt on Google in a private window. AI Overviews appear above the results for some searches, not all of them: write "no overview" when there is none. Then ask the same prompt in AI Mode, available in the UK since July 2025. Google's AI answers draw on Google Search, so they are the ones your usual SEO work affects most directly.

{{< screenshot id="check-ai-visibility-04" alt="Google AI Mode answering a local service prompt, with the businesses and links it cites" >}}

If your customers use Microsoft Copilot or Claude, add them under the same rule: no history, no memory.

**Location matters.** Assistants adapt local answers to where they think you are. ChatGPT search, for one, can pass a general location based on your IP address to the search providers it uses ([OpenAI's help article on ChatGPT search](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/9237897-chatgpt-search)). Run the test from your own area, or keep the town in every prompt.

## Step 4: record what each answer says

Fill in each row as soon as the answer appears: by the fifth answer about office cleaning, they all start to blur.

- **Named:** Yes only if your business name appears. A link to a directory that lists you, without your name, counts as No.
- **What it said:** copy the sentence about you exactly, mistakes included, then compare it with your current services, area, prices, hours and phone number.
- **Sources:** note the domains cited: directories, review sites, local press, forum threads, your own site.

I check the Accurate column closely with ChatGPT. In my experience it makes things up a bit too often, which is one reason I use Claude for my own work. An invented service or an old phone number can usually be traced to a source, and a source can be corrected.

Then ask your five most important prompts a second time, in a new chat and on a new row. Answers vary from one run to the next, and a second run shows whether a mention is stable or a fluke.

## Step 5: work out your mention rate each month

Your mention rate is the share of answers that name you. Work it out for each assistant separately, because each one draws on different sources. With the assistant in column B and Yes or No in column D, this formula gives ChatGPT's rate:

```
=COUNTIFS(B:B,"ChatGPT",D:D,"Yes")/COUNTIF(B:B,"ChatGPT")
```

Format the cell as a percentage and copy the formula for each assistant, changing the name. A rate of 30% means you were named in three answers out of ten.

{{< screenshot id="check-ai-visibility-05" alt="Monthly tab of the AI visibility spreadsheet with the mention rate per assistant below the rows" >}}

Keep a summary tab with one line per month: the mention rate for each assistant, the number of inaccurate answers and the competitor named most often. Read it over three months rather than one. The same competitor named month after month, or the same mistake coming back, is a pattern worth acting on; a single odd answer is not.

## Free reports from Microsoft and Google

Microsoft and Google now publish some AI data about your site for free. It covers their own AI features, so it says little about ChatGPT, the Gemini app or Perplexity, but it adds real figures to your manual test:

| Tool | What it shows | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Bing Webmaster Tools, AI Performance report | How often Copilot and Bing's AI summaries cite your pages, and the grounding queries behind those citations | In preview since February 2026; sampled data, no clicks |
| Microsoft Clarity, Citations report | How often AI answers cite your site, mainly in Copilot, over 7, 28 or 90 days | Relies on Microsoft's ecosystem |
| Google Search Console, generative AI report | How often AI Overviews and AI Mode show your pages | Impressions only: no clicks, no queries |

Grounding queries are the searches an assistant runs before it answers. They show which words led Copilot to your pages, and they make good prompts for your own test. Clarity's Citations report is free for every Clarity user ([Microsoft's announcement](https://clarity.microsoft.com/blog/citations-now-generally-available/)).

## Paid trackers: Semrush and Squarespace

Paid trackers run your prompts on a schedule and chart how often you are named. They save time and handle more prompts than you would by hand; read their figures as a trend, as you would your spreadsheet. Run the free test for three months before paying for one, though: it is easy for a small team to fall for every new tool, and the channels that already bring you clients come first.

Semrush, owned by Adobe since April 2026, sells an AI Visibility Toolkit on its own and inside Semrush One, its bundle with the SEO Toolkit. ## What to do with your results

| What you see | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| No assistant names you | They cannot reach your site, or find nothing on it that answers the prompt | Check that AI crawlers can reach your site, then give each service and town a clear page |
| Named, with wrong details | Old or conflicting information somewhere | Correct the sources cited, then test again a few weeks later |
| Named, but without a link to your site | Other sites answer the question better than yours | Add the missing facts to your own pages, written to be quoted |
| Competitors named instead of you | They appear in sources where you are missing | Get listed, reviewed or written about on those sites |
| Named by one assistant, not another | Each one searches a different index | Make sure Google and Bing both index your site |

The first two rows are the groundwork in my guide on how to {{< a key="get-recommended-by-chatgpt" >}}get recommended by ChatGPT{{< /a >}}: crawler access, one page per service and town, and identical details on your site, your Google Business Profile and your directory listings. For the third row, my tutorial on how to {{< a key="write-content-for-ai-search" >}}write content for AI search{{< /a >}} shows how to write pages that assistants quote.

The Competitors column is the most useful one. For social media, I like Metricool because it lets you watch what competitors do, especially the ones that are succeeding, and your spreadsheet works the same way. Take the competitor named most often and open the sources cited next to it: a directory, a review site, a local news article, a forum thread. Those are the places to be next.

Mentions on other sites take the longest to earn and count the most. Start with your town's paper, then the regional press, then national titles. My guide on {{< a key="get-press-coverage" >}}how to get press coverage{{< /a >}} explains how to pitch a journalist.

## Your monthly AI visibility check

1. Duplicate last month's tab and clear the answers, keeping the ten prompts.
2. Ask each prompt in ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google, logged out or without memory, in a new chat each time.
3. Fill in every row, then ask your five main prompts a second time.
4. Work out the mention rate for each assistant and note the competitor named most often.
5. Look at Bing's AI Performance report, Clarity's Citations report and Search Console's generative AI report.
6. Choose one fix for the month: a page, a correction, a directory listing or a pitch to a journalist.
