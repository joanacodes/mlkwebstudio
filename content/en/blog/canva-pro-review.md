Canva Pro is the paid version of Canva for one person, and at the time of writing (October 2026) it costs about £100 a year in the UK (US$120 in the US). My answer to "is Canva Pro worth it?" depends on how often you open Canva. For a small business that makes visuals every week, usually yes: it pays for itself as soon as it saves you a few minutes a week. For a business that posts a few times a month, the free plan does the job, and the money is better spent elsewhere.

I use Canva every day for little tasks: not having Canva for my work would be like not having a kettle in the house. For heavy design work, such as a logo or a print brochure, I switch to Affinity or the Adobe suite. So the useful question is not whether to use Canva, but whether the paid plan adds enough to the free one.

Below, I compare what the free plan covers with what Pro adds, give the current prices, list the signs that Pro pays off and explain how to test it on a real month of work. Canva is one of the tools in my guide to {{< a key="small-business-tools-guide" >}}small business marketing tools{{< /a >}}.

## What Canva's free plan already covers

Canva's free plan is not a cut-down demo. You work in the same editor as paying users, on the formats a small business needs most: social posts and stories, Pinterest Pins, presentations, flyers, short videos and simple documents. At the time of writing, the free plan includes:

- a library of free templates, photos and graphics, large enough for everyday visuals;
- one **Brand Kit**, where you keep your logo, colours and fonts;
- 5 GB of cloud storage;
- Canva's AI tools, with a smaller allowance than on the paid plans;
- sharing, so a colleague or a client can comment on or edit a design;
- downloads in the usual formats, such as PNG, JPG and PDF.

The single Brand Kit matters more than it looks. It keeps your logo, colours and fonts one click away, and that is most of what makes visuals look consistent from one post to the next. A business with one brand does not need Pro for that.

### Where the free plan stops

The limits show once you post often:

- Premium templates, photos, videos and graphics are reserved for the paid plans.
- The time-saving tools are paid: resizing a design into other formats, translating it and removing a photo's background.
- 5 GB fills up quickly if you edit video.

## What Canva Pro adds, and what it costs in October 2026

Canva Pro is the paid plan for one person. At the time of writing (October 2026), it costs about £100 a year in the UK, or about £13 a month if you pay monthly. Canva's US page lists the yearly plan at US$120. Canva sets its prices country by country and changes them from time to time, so check the figure on [Canva's pricing page](https://www.canva.com/pricing/) before you pay.

Paying for the year saves money: twelve months at £13 come to £156, against about £100 for the year. Choose yearly billing only once you know you will use Pro, which is what the free trial is for.

| | Free | Pro |
|---|---|---|
| Price in the UK (October 2026) | £0 | About £100 a year or £13 a month |
| Templates, photos, videos, graphics | Free library | Free library plus the premium library |
| Brand Kits | One | Several, for more than one brand |
| Cloud storage | 5 GB | Much more |
| Resize a design into other formats | No | Yes |
| Translate a design | No | Yes |
| Remove a photo's background | No | Yes |
| AI tools | Smaller allowance | Higher allowance |

These are the features that matter to most small businesses. The full list is longer and changes from year to year, as Canva adds AI tools and moves features between plans, so the pricing page has the final word.

Above Pro, Canva sells **Business**, for teams, and **Enterprise**, for large organisations. I come back to Business below.

## Is Canva Pro worth it? The signs it pays for itself

At about £100 a year, Pro costs less than £2 a week. If an hour of your time is worth £30, it pays for itself as soon as it saves you four minutes a week. The real question is whether you would use what it adds. These are the signs that you would:

- **You publish several times a week.** A café that posts its specials every morning, or a tradesperson who shows every finished job, opens Canva often enough for premium templates and photos to save real time.
- **You post the same message in several formats.** Resizing turns a square post into a vertical Pin or a story in a few clicks; you then adjust the layout instead of rebuilding it. That matters if you put your effort into Pinterest and YouTube Shorts, probably the best allies of a small business in 2026, because a post on either keeps bringing views long after you publish it. My guide to {{< a key="youtube-shorts-for-business" >}}YouTube Shorts for business{{< /a >}} shows how to make them with simple tools.
- **You photograph products, dishes or people.** The background remover cuts out the subject in one click, a job that takes ages by hand and rarely looks clean.
- **You sell in two languages or run two brands.** Translation gives you a first draft of your flyer in French or English (have a fluent speaker check it), and several Brand Kits keep two brands apart.
- **You use Canva's AI tools every week.** Pro's allowance is higher than the free one, which matters if you generate images or text in Canva regularly.

The rule of thumb I would apply: a tool you open every day for small tasks earns its place in the budget. A subscription you open twice a year is a gym membership with nicer fonts.

## When the free plan is enough

Be honest about how you work before you upgrade. The free plan is enough when:

- **You post a few times a month.** Free templates and one Brand Kit cover a handful of posts, a monthly offer and the odd flyer.
- **You have your own photos.** Photos of your real work, team and premises convince more than stock images that any competitor can download too. When I build a website and the client has no pictures, I generate or buy them, but a business's own photos are the better starting point.
- **What you need most is scheduling.** If the goal is to plan a month of posts, a dedicated scheduler is a better first purchase. Metricool, which I rate highly, schedules your posts, shows what your most successful competitors publish and tracks your own growth, and its first tier covers a generous number of accounts. - **The low price is the main attraction.** It is easy to fall for a new tool because it looks cheap, and small subscriptions add up. That is {{< a key="shiny-object-syndrome" >}}shiny object syndrome{{< /a >}}: get the most from the channels and tools you already use before adding another. AppSumo plays on the same feeling, which is why I describe it as a bit of a casino in my {{< a key="appsumo-review" >}}AppSumo review{{< /a >}}.

## Canva Pro, Business or a professional tool

If two or three people make visuals for your business, compare Pro with **Canva Business**. It is priced per person, costs more per person than Pro and is built for teams that share a brand. Enterprise is for large organisations; most small businesses can ignore it.

Some limits stay whatever you pay. Canva is a template tool: for a logo, a full visual identity, a print catalogue or serious photo retouching, a professional app does the job better. Affinity, which Canva relaunched as a free app in October 2025, is the natural next step, and Adobe remains the standard if you exchange files with designers. I compare the three in {{< a key="canva-vs-adobe-vs-affinity" >}}Canva vs Adobe vs Affinity{{< /a >}}.

A visual identity is heavy work, and it comes first in my {{< a page="/services/websites" >}}website projects{{< /a >}}. Once the logo, colours and fonts exist, the daily visuals can go back to Canva, where the Brand Kit applies them.

{{< callout type="note" >}}Paying for Pro does not give you a logo of your own. Canva says a logo made from its templates is not exclusive to you and cannot be registered as a trademark: start from a blank page with simple shapes and fonts, or work with a designer. Details on [Canva's help page about logos and trademarks](https://www.canva.com/en_gb/help/trademarks-logo/).{{< /callout >}}

## How to test Canva Pro on a real month of work

The 30-day free trial answers the question with your own numbers rather than mine.

1. **List what you make in a typical month**: posts, Pins, Shorts, flyers, quotes, presentations.
2. **Start the trial at the beginning of a busy month**, not in a quiet week, so the test reflects real work.
3. **Set a reminder a few days before it ends.** The trial turns into a paid plan unless you cancel.
4. **Note each time a Pro feature saves you time**: a resize, a background removed, a premium photo you would otherwise have searched for. Write down the minutes.
5. **Do the maths at the end.** Divide the yearly price by 52 and compare it with what the minutes saved each week are worth to you. If Pro saves you less than about four minutes a week at £30 an hour, stay on the free plan.
6. **If you keep Pro, switch to yearly billing**, now that you know you will use it.
7. **If you stop, download the final versions** of designs that use premium elements before the plan ends.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}Do not judge the AI tools during the trial. Canva says a Pro trial comes with the **same AI allowance as the free plan**; the higher allowance starts once the trial converts to a paid plan.{{< /callout >}}

The free plan used every week beats a Pro subscription you forget to open. Upgrade when the trial shows that Pro saves you time every week, and not before.
