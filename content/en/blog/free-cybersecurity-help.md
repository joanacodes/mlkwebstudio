You can get a surprising amount of free cyber security help as a small-team business, and almost all of it comes from public bodies: guides written for teams without an IT department, a self-assessment tool, practice exercises, threat alerts, a free diagnostic in France and someone to call while an attack is under way. What you pay for is certification and the hands-on repair work.

This guide lists what is free in the UK, where most of it comes from the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), explains why Cyber Essentials is not on that list, and sums up the French services (17Cyber, Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr and MonAideCyber) for teams with clients or staff on both sides of the Channel. Everything was checked on 19 October 2026. It is part of my {{< a key="cybersecurity-guide" >}}cybersecurity guide for small teams{{< /a >}}.

When a client calls me with a problem, part of my job is telling them honestly whether they need me or someone else. For much of what follows, that someone else is a free public service.

## What free cyber security help covers, and what it doesn't

Public help comes in four kinds, and knowing which one you need saves an afternoon of clicking around government websites.

- **Guidance**: what to do, in plain language, written for organisations without IT staff.
- **Tools and practice**: a self-assessment that turns the guidance into a to-do list, and exercises to rehearse an incident with your team.
- **A person who looks at your set-up**: in France, MonAideCyber pairs you with a trained volunteer for a free diagnostic. In the UK, the closest thing is the funded Cyber Essentials support that some organisations qualify for.
- **Help during an attack**: somewhere to report the crime and get advice, at any hour.

None of these services will fix your systems, certify you, give you legal advice or insure you. Those parts are paid, and I come back to them at the end.

## The UK: what the NCSC and the police give you for free

The NCSC, part of GCHQ, publishes most of the free help, at no cost and without a sales call. The police side is handled by Report Fraud.

| Free service | What you get | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| [Small organisations guide to cyber security](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/small-organisations-guide-to-cyber-security) | Advice on five areas: email, important online accounts, devices, backups and spotting attacks | Everyone: start here |
| Small Business Guide: Response & Recovery | How to prepare for an incident and plan your recovery | Writing your incident plan |
| Cyber Action Toolkit | Small steps tailored to your organisation, done at your own pace, with your progress recorded | Sole traders and small teams who want a to-do list |
| Exercise in a Box | Realistic scenarios to practise how your team responds to an incident | Teams that want to rehearse before the real thing |
| Early Warning | Alerts about threats tied to your organisation's IP addresses | Businesses with a fixed IP address or their own servers |
| [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) | Reporting of fraud and cyber crime, online or on 0300 123 2040 | Any attack or scam |

### The guide and the Cyber Action Toolkit

The guide for small organisations is the April 2026 edition of what used to be the NCSC's Small Business Guide, and the old address still leads to it. Read it first: it is short, and it is written for people who do not work in IT.

If you would rather work from a checklist, the Cyber Action Toolkit breaks the same advice into small steps tailored to your organisation, which you complete at your own pace. It records your progress and points you towards Cyber Essentials when you are ready, so nothing you do there is wasted if a client later asks for the certificate.

### Exercise in a Box and Early Warning

Exercise in a Box gives you realistic incident scenarios to rehearse with your team. Run one as an hour-long discussion with the people who would be on the phone during a real incident: you find out who has the admin passwords, who calls the bank and who talks to clients before it matters. The NCSC added a scheme of assured companies for bespoke exercises in 2026, but Exercise in a Box remains free.

Early Warning sends free alerts about threats tied to your organisation's IP addresses. Sign-up takes a few minutes, and findings arrive through a MyNCSC account. It is most useful if your business has a fixed IP address or its own servers; a team that works only from laptops and cloud apps gets less out of it.

{{< callout type="note" >}}**Web Check and Mail Check have gone.** The NCSC retired both on 31 March 2026, saying commercial tools now cover the same ground. If an older article sends you there, Early Warning is the free NCSC alert service that carries on.{{< /callout >}}

### Report Fraud, the 24/7 line and Cyber Resilience Centres

Report Fraud replaced Action Fraud on 4 December 2025. It takes reports of fraud and cyber crime from individuals and businesses in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, online or on 0300 123 2040; in Scotland, call Police Scotland on 101. If an attack is still in progress, call the same number at any hour: it is also the 24/7 line for organisations under attack. For a significant attack, the NCSC asks you to report it there first.

You may also have a regional Cyber Resilience Centre nearby. These police-led, not-for-profit centres have offered small organisations free core membership, with guidance, threat updates and a named contact. The terms differ from one centre to the next and I found no 2026 update, so check the one for your area before counting on it.

## Cyber Essentials is not free: what it costs and what you get

Cyber Essentials is the government-backed certification, run by the NCSC with its delivery partner IASME. It checks five controls: firewalls, secure configuration, security update management, user access control and malware protection. You answer a self-assessment, a director signs it off and an independent assessor marks it.

**The certificate itself is paid.** At the time of writing (October 2026), IASME charges £320 plus VAT (about $425) for an organisation with up to nine employees and £440 plus VAT (about $585) for 10 to 49. Cyber Essentials Plus, the audited version, is quoted by the certification body. The [NCSC's Cyber Essentials page](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/cyberessentials/overview) has the current requirements and fees.

What is free around it:

- **The preparation.** The requirements, the NCSC's guidance and IASME's readiness tool cost nothing, and the Cyber Action Toolkit leads into the same five controls.
- **Insurance.** Organisations based in the UK or the Crown Dependencies, with a turnover under £20 million, that certify their whole organisation can opt in to cyber liability insurance arranged by IASME at no extra cost. My guide to {{< a key="cyber-insurance" >}}cyber insurance{{< /a >}} explains what a policy covers and where a free one stops.
- **Funded support, for some.** The NCSC has run a Funded Cyber Essentials Programme that paid for about 20 hours of help from an assessor for eligible micro and small organisations, such as legal-aid firms and some small charities. Eligibility has changed between rounds and I could not confirm that a round is open in October 2026, so check IASME's website before counting on it.

Pay for the certificate when a client asks for it or when you want the insurance. The NCSC encourages large organisations to set Cyber Essentials as a minimum for some suppliers, so the question often arrives inside a {{< a key="security-questionnaire" >}}security questionnaire{{< /a >}}, and EU clients covered by {{< a key="nis2-small-business" >}}NIS2{{< /a >}} must also manage the security of their supply chain (Article 21 of the directive). If nobody has asked yet, spend the first month on the free toolkit: the controls are the same, and the certificate can follow when someone needs to see it.

## France: 17Cyber, Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr and MonAideCyber

If you have clients, staff or an office in France, the French state's free help is more hands-on than the UK's: it includes a diagnostic with a person and regional teams that respond to incidents.

| Service | What it does |
|---|---|
| [17Cyber](https://17cyber.gouv.fr/) | Launched in December 2024 by the police, the gendarmerie and Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr. Answer a few questions online, at any hour, and get advice for your case; for a serious attack, chat with a police officer or gendarme and start a complaint. |
| Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr | The national service for victims of cybercrime: practical guides, an awareness kit for staff, and providers it labels ExpertCyber for paid repairs. In June 2026 it announced MalletteCyber Pro, a toolkit for small and medium-sized companies. |
| MonAideCyber | A free diagnostic of about an hour and a half with a volunteer trained by ANSSI, France's national cyber security agency. It covers six themes, and you leave with six measures to apply within six months, with a follow-up six months later. |
| Regional CSIRTs | Free first-level response to an incident for companies, local authorities and associations in their region, then referral to a specialist provider. |

If you run a French office from the UK, save the 17Cyber address next to the UK numbers: the French services are the ones that can help with an incident on that side of the Channel.

## Where to start: a free plan for a small team

1. **Today: save the numbers.** Put your bank's fraud line and 0300 123 2040 in your phone, plus 17cyber.gouv.fr if you have a French office. My {{< a key="incident-response-first-hour" >}}first-hour plan after a cyber attack{{< /a >}} explains what to do with them.
2. **This week: read the NCSC guide or start the Cyber Action Toolkit.** The first actions take minutes each: {{< a key="two-factor-authentication-passkeys" >}}two-factor authentication{{< /a >}} on email and banking, backups that run on their own, updates on every laptop and phone (my {{< a key="device-security-basics" >}}device security basics{{< /a >}} have the checklist).
3. **This quarter: run one Exercise in a Box scenario** with the people who would handle an incident, then fix the gaps it shows.
4. **If you have a fixed IP address or your own servers, sign up for Early Warning.**
5. **When a client asks for proof, certify with Cyber Essentials** and reuse the same evidence in their questionnaire.
6. **For a French office, request a MonAideCyber diagnostic** and put the six measures in the diary.

## What free help won't do, and when to pay someone

Public services advise, diagnose, alert and refer. None of them will sign in to your systems to clean them, rebuild a hacked website or fill in a client's questionnaire for you. 17Cyber and Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr can point you to a labelled provider, but that provider's work is paid.

Pay for hands-on help when:

- ransomware has reached several computers, or an attacker still controls a mailbox or an admin account;
- your website has been hacked and you cannot get back in;
- a client contract asks you to prove more than Cyber Essentials covers.

In the UK, companies assured under the NCSC's Cyber Incident Response scheme handle serious incidents, and its Standard level covers most of them; in France, look for the ExpertCyber label. If you have cyber insurance, call its helpline first, as the insurer may want its own responders.

This article is not legal advice: for a contract clause, a personal data breach or the question of whether NIS2 applies to you, a lawyer or a data protection officer is worth paying for.

Hacked websites, lost admin access and mailboxes you cannot get back into are the IT rescue work I do, usually starting the same day or within 24 hours. For the rest, start with the free services above: I would rather send you there than sell you something you can get for nothing.
