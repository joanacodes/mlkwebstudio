Many of the lead magnet ideas you find online come from people who sell online courses, and they chase volume. A service business needs the opposite: fewer sign-ups, from people who could hire you this year, and a reason to talk to them.

Below are 12 ideas, each with an example from a different trade, then how to deliver, promote and measure yours. This guide belongs to my {{< a key="funnels-newsletters-guide" >}}complete guide to sales funnels and newsletters{{< /a >}}.

## What is a lead magnet?

**A lead magnet is a free, useful resource that people get in exchange for their contact details, so that you can follow up with those who are interested but not ready to buy.** It works in the middle of the funnel, as my explanation of {{< a key="sales-funnel-explained" >}}how a sales funnel works for a service business{{< /a >}} shows: the visitor has found you but is not ready to call.

For a shop, it is often a discount code. For a service, it is a slice of your expertise that people can use without you.

## What makes a good lead magnet for a service business

Four tests. A lead magnet that fails one still collects addresses, just not the right ones.

1. **Specific.** One problem, one type of client: "20 checks before a new tenant moves in" attracts landlords, "marketing tips" attracts everyone. I learnt this with websites: for one client, a site offering too many building trades did not work at all, while the one selling a few very specific services ranks.
2. **A quick win.** Usable in under 30 minutes, with a result at the end: a score, a figure, a yes or a no. A one-page checklist that gets used beats a 40-page ebook nobody opens.
3. **Close to what you sell.** Hiring you is the natural next step: a cleaning price calculator leads to a cleaning quote.
4. **It qualifies.** The topic, the wording and sometimes one extra question on the form (team size, postcode) tell you who signed up. Ask only for what you will use.

## 12 lead magnet ideas for service businesses, with a sector example each

The examples are illustrations, not client stories: swap the trade for yours and keep the shape.

### 1. A checklist

What a careful professional checks, in order. *Example:* a letting agent offers "20 checks before a new tenant moves in" to landlords who manage their own flats; those who find the list long are the likeliest to hand over the management.

### 2. An audit or a scorecard

Questions ending with a score and what it means. *Example:* a health and safety consultant offers "Is your risk assessment up to date? 15 questions, a score out of 15". A low score is a reason to book a visit.

### 3. A template

A document people would otherwise build from scratch. *Example:* an interior designer offers a room-by-room renovation budget spreadsheet, which shows how many decisions a renovation involves: the designer's job.

### 4. A price calculator

A few questions, then a price range: it answers every prospect's first question and filters out budgets you cannot serve. *Example:* a commercial cleaning company asks for the floor area, visits a week and postcode, shows a monthly range and offers a detailed estimate by email.

### 5. An email mini-course

Five short emails over a week or two, one idea each, so your name stays in the inbox. *Example:* a commercial property solicitor sends "Five things to check in a commercial lease before you sign"; the last email offers a fixed-fee lease review.

### 6. A case study pack

Three projects told the way a buyer thinks (problem, budget range, timeline, result, photos), with your clients' permission. *Example:* a garden designer gathers three gardens, from a courtyard to a large plot, with a price bracket for each.

### 7. A swipe file

Finished examples to copy, where a template is a blank to fill in. *Example:* a bookkeeper offers "10 payment reminder emails, from friendly to final notice"; owners who chase invoices often are ready-made clients.

### 8. A benchmark

Figures nobody else publishes, from your own work, anonymised and aggregated. *Example:* a recruitment agency publishes salary ranges for ten roles in Manchester, from its placements of the past year.

### 9. A workshop recording

A 30 to 45-minute session with slides; ask participants before you share it. *Example:* an HR consultant records "Running your first staff appraisals" for managers of teams of five to twenty.

### 10. A 15-minute diagnostic

A short call with a fixed scope: one question answered, no pitch. *Example:* an architect offers homeowners 15 minutes to say whether their project needs planning permission. A booking page with two or three questions does the filtering: I prefer YouCanBookMe, and Calendly is good too.

### 11. A quiz

A few questions leading to results that each point to a different service. *Example:* an IT support company asks "Which backup set-up fits your team?"; each of its three results gets its own follow-up email.

### 12. A local guide

Practical information about one place, which national competitors cannot match. *Example:* a removals company publishes a guide to moving in Bristol: parking for the van, recycling centres, council pages for bins and council tax.

## How to choose yours

Start from the questions prospects ask on the first call. Mine ask whether I have experience, what SEO will do for them and how long it takes to rank: each could become a resource. When I write copy, I also use Claude and deep research to find what people in a niche complain about most; a complaint that keeps coming back in reviews is a topic people will trade an email address for.

Keep the ideas that pass the four tests and make one, not five. It is easy to fall for a shiny new format every month; one lead magnet placed well does more than five nobody sees.

## How to deliver it: a form, a confirmation email, a link

You do not need a funnel builder: Brevo, MailerLite, Mailchimp and Kit all offer sign-up forms with double opt-in.

1. **A page and a form.** One page per lead magnet, and the same form at the end of related articles. Ask for an email address, and a first name only if you will use it.
2. **A confirmation email.** The person clicks to confirm the address (double opt-in), then gets the resource. Typos and fake addresses stay off your list, and you keep a record of each sign-up.
3. **A link, not an attachment**, to a file you can correct without sending anything again.
4. **A short follow-up** for those who tick the newsletter box (see below): two or three emails ending with an invitation to book a call, as in my {{< a key="welcome-email-sequence" >}}welcome email sequence{{< /a >}}.

No article of the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679) requires double opt-in, but Article 7(1) requires you to be able to show that a person consented, and a confirmation click is the simplest proof.

### The newsletter is a separate choice

Sending the resource to someone who asked for it is one thing; adding them to your newsletter is another, which usually needs its own yes. Put an unticked box under the form ("Also send me the monthly newsletter") and deliver the resource either way.

Making the newsletter the price of the download is risky. Under Article 7(4) and Recital 43 of the GDPR (Article 7(4) is the same in the UK GDPR), consent is presumed not to be free when a service depends on it without needing it, and the EDPB's consent guidelines put the burden of proof on you. The [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-electronic-mail-marketing-rules/) takes the same line for email marketing, while allowing incentives if people who refuse are not unfairly penalised. As of 19 October 2026, I found no text from the CNIL, France's regulator, on downloads as such; its general rule is a real choice, with no negative consequence for saying no.

Business-to-business rules are looser: no prior consent is needed to [email limited companies in the UK](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/), or professionals about their work in France, provided they were told and can opt out. But a form rarely tells you who signed up in which capacity, so the separate box stays the simplest route. The rules also follow your readers: a UK, Swiss or US business whose resource targets people in the EU falls under the EU GDPR for those sign-ups (Article 3(2)).

My guide to {{< a key="newsletter-consent-gdpr" >}}newsletter consent, soft opt-in and double opt-in{{< /a >}} covers the details. This is a summary, not legal advice: if your form feeds several lists or partners, have it checked by a lawyer or your DPO.

## Where to promote it, cold emails included

- **At the end of related articles**, where the reader has just spent five minutes on that problem.
- **On its own page**, linked from the matching service page and the footer.
- **On LinkedIn.** Put the most useful part in the post itself and add the page to the Featured section of your profile. Skip "comment Yes and I'll send it": LinkedIn said in March 2026 that its feed would show less of that kind of engagement bait.
- **At events**, with a QR code on your last slide, and **in your email signature**.

### In cold emails: offer it, do not link it

I never put links in cold emails: plain text with a phone number is what gets my emails delivered and answered, as I explain in {{< a key="plain-text-cold-emails" >}}why I send plain text only{{< /a >}}. So offer the resource, and send it to the people who reply:

```
Subject: Office cleaning in Croydon

Hello [first name],
We clean offices of 10 to 50 people in Croydon.
I have a one-page checklist of what a cleaning contract should cover: shall I send it to you?
Or call me on [your phone number].

[Your name]
```

When I run campaigns for clients, I do everything except replying, so the request reaches the person who sells. Treat the offer line like any copy: I A/B test my campaigns, and responses improve as I learn which wording works.

## How to measure it: sign-up rate per placement

- **Sign-up rate = sign-ups from one placement ÷ views of that placement.** An article read 800 times in a month whose form brings 12 sign-ups converts 1.5% of its readers (invented figures, to show the maths).
- **One form or tag per placement**, so your newsletter tool shows where each subscriber came from; for links shared on LinkedIn or at events, add UTM parameters, as in my tutorial on {{< a key="utm-ga4-funnel-tracking" >}}UTM links and GA4{{< /a >}}.
- **Calls and quotes.** Count the subscribers who book a call or ask for a quote within three months.

Readers but no sign-ups: change the promise (the title and the line under it) before the design. Sign-ups but no calls: the topic is too far from what you sell.

## Next steps: your first lead magnet this month

1. Write down the five questions prospects ask most often on a first call.
2. Pick the idea that answers one of them and sits closest to your paid service, and draft it with your own figures.
3. Build the page, the form with double opt-in, the newsletter box and two or three follow-up emails.
4. Place it at the end of related articles, on the service page, on LinkedIn and in your signature.
5. After a month, fix the weakest placement before you make a second lead magnet.
