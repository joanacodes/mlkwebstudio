A small business website does not need to be big or clever. It has three jobs: get you found when people search for what you sell, convince them within seconds that you are worth calling, and make that call easy. Every other choice, from the platform to the colour of the buttons, serves those three jobs.

This guide is the hub of my websites section. It covers the type of site you need, the platform that suits the person who will edit it, what it costs, how it gets built and launched, and how to keep it working once it is live. Each part links to a detailed article when you want to go further.

I build websites for small businesses in France and the UK. I will also tell you plainly when you do not need someone like me.

## What a small business website has to do

Before you look at a single template, be clear about what the site is for. For a small business, it comes down to three jobs.

1. Get found. People type a service and a town into Google, and some now ask an AI assistant such as ChatGPT or Gemini to recommend someone. A page for each service, written in the words your clients use, gives both something to rank and something to quote. My {{< a key="small-business-seo-guide" >}}small business SEO guide{{< /a >}} covers the method, and the {{< a key="ai-search-geo-guide" >}}guide to generative engine optimisation{{< /a >}} covers the AI side.
2. Get taken seriously. Visitors judge how a site looks in about 50 milliseconds, and that first impression colours how credible they find you. I want my clients' prospects to be a little intimidated by the website, because a business that looks established gets haggled with less. Here is {{< a key="professional-looking-website" >}}how to make your website look professional{{< /a >}}.
3. Turn visits into enquiries. A phone number you can tap, a short form and one clear next step on every page. If people visit but never get in touch, the leak is usually on the page itself, and {{< a key="website-conversion-rate" >}}why a website gets visits but no enquiries{{< /a >}} shows how to find it.

**A site that does these three jobs well beats a bigger site that does them badly.** Keep that test in mind for every decision below.

## Which type of website do you need?

Most small businesses need one of four kinds of site, and the choice shapes everything after it.

| Type | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Brochure website | A few pages presenting the business, with one page per service | Trades, consultants, local services, most B2B firms |
| Landing page | One page built around one offer and one action | A paid campaign, a launch, an event |
| Online shop | A catalogue with a basket and online payment | Selling products online |
| Web app | A tool people log in to and use | Client portals, quoting tools, internal tools |

A brochure website is what most of my clients need first, because prospects check it before they call. Landing pages come on top of it, one per campaign, and {{< a key="landing-page-vs-website" >}}landing page vs website{{< /a >}} explains when a single page is enough. Edenel Nettoyage, a cleaning company in Paris, had no website at all: I built it a brochure site first, and the SEO comes next.

If what you need is a tool for your own team, such as a place to track enquiries and jobs, that is an app rather than a website. I have described {{< a key="bespoke-crm-internal-app" >}}the internal app I built when a client's leads piled up{{< /a >}}.

### One site, one clear offer

## Choose the platform by who will edit the site

Website builders' feature lists all look alike. The question that decides is simpler: once the site is live, who will change a price, swap a photo or add a new service?

| Platform | Suits | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Squarespace | An owner who will edit the site themselves | Closed: no access to the source code, and only a partial export |
| WordPress | A business with the budget for a specialist every month | Core, theme and plugin updates, and the security that comes with them |
| Wix Studio | A business that hires a Wix specialist | Hard to leave: Wix sites cannot be exported to another host |
| Webflow | A business with a Webflow developer, or plenty of time | Not really no-code, and a steep learning curve |
| AI-built static site | A hands-on owner who is comfortable with files | No visual editor: every change goes through the files |
| Custom-coded site | A business that pays a developer for changes | Every edit goes through the developer |

### Squarespace: you will edit the site yourself

Every client who has come to me from WordPress wanted to leave it: the learning curve, the plugins, the fear of changing one word and watching the layout fall apart. Owners who run their own site should not have to struggle like that, so I move them to Squarespace. **It is the easiest website builder I know**: hosting, security and software updates are included, and the owner makes changes whenever they like, without calling anyone.

Its weakness is that it is closed. You get no access to the source code, and it exports only part of your content. Custom-coded, Webflow or WordPress sites may rank higher for that reason, yet the Squarespace site I built for Pilotech ranks first or second on Google. Get the SEO basics right, meaning the titles, the descriptions and the right keywords, and the platform will not be what holds you back; {{< a key="squarespace-seo" >}}Squarespace SEO{{< /a >}} lists the settings.

I often add a little custom code to the Squarespace sites I build, nothing that gets in the way of the owner's edits, and they call me only for that part. Code injection needs the Core plan or higher, not Basic, and {{< a key="squarespace-code-injection" >}}Squarespace code injection{{< /a >}} shows how to add code without breaking anything. To build the site yourself, follow my {{< a key="squarespace-tutorial" >}}Squarespace tutorial{{< /a >}}; for the full comparison, read {{< a key="squarespace-vs-wordpress" >}}Squarespace vs WordPress{{< /a >}}.

### WordPress: a big budget and a specialist every month

WordPress can do almost anything, which is why so many developers and agencies use it. The price of that freedom is upkeep: the core, the theme and every plugin need updating, and plugins are where most of the risk sits. [Patchstack counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025](https://www.patchstack.com/whitepaper/state-of-wordpress-security-in-2026/), about 91 per cent of them in plugins. Pilotech first called me in an emergency because its WordPress site had been hacked; I recovered it, then built the company a new website.

I usually discourage solo owners from WordPress. The learning curve is steep, and they end up having to become their own webmaster. If you have a large budget and a specialist who looks after the site every month, WordPress is a fine choice: you get the control, and someone else does the upkeep.

### Wix Studio and Webflow: only with a specialist

On paper, Wix is the best website builder there is. In use, I have always found it laggy, on different devices, connections and countries. I have tried every one of its platforms and even attended its events, and solo business owners are the ones who fall into the trap. And yet I have seen web designers who specialise in Wix build great sites with it. So if you choose Wix, choose Wix Studio, hire a specialist and do not plan to touch the site yourself. Wix launched a third editor, Wix Harmony, in January 2026, with an AI agent built into drag-and-drop editing; {{< a key="wix-platforms-history" >}}Wix Studio vs Wix Editor vs Harmony{{< /a >}} untangles the lot.

Webflow is good only if you have a Webflow specialist you can pay, or a lot of time and patience, because the learning curve is very steep. It is not really no-code: you need to understand how a website is coded to use it well, and its real audience is web developers. The fact that there is a Webflow University tells you a lot.

### An AI-built static site: you like doing it yourself

If you are hands-on and happy working with files, there is now a simpler route than Webflow. Ask an AI assistant to write a static site in plain HTML and CSS, check every word, then host it on GitHub Pages, Spaceship or another host. GitHub Pages costs nothing, so the domain is your only fixed cost; this website runs on Hugo and GitHub Pages. The limits are real, though: there is no visual editor, every change goes through the files, and GitHub's rules make Pages a fit for a brochure site, not a shop.

{{< a key="build-website-with-ai" >}}How to build a website with AI{{< /a >}} walks through the brief, the files, the hosting and the domain, and {{< a key="spaceship-hosting" >}}Spaceship hosting explained{{< /a >}} covers a paid alternative.

A custom-coded site works the same way, except that a developer makes the changes, not you. It is what I build for clients with the budget to pay me every month, or whenever they want something changed; most of Pilotech's sites are custom-coded.

## What a website costs to build and run

A website costs money twice: once to build it, then every year to keep it online and up to date. Here are the main routes, with what each asks of your budget and of your time.

| Route | What you pay | What it asks of you |
|---|---|---|
| Squarespace, built by you | A subscription, plus the domain | Time to learn and build, then regular edits |
| AI-built static site | The domain only, if GitHub Pages hosts it | Comfort with files, and every change by hand |
| Built for you on Squarespace | A one-off project fee, then the subscription | Your texts, photos and feedback |
| WordPress with a specialist | A project fee, hosting, then a monthly fee for upkeep | Little, as long as the specialist stays |

A .co.uk domain costs $5.42 (about £4) a year at Spaceship, and the renewal costs the same.

## Domain, hosting and email: how the pieces connect

Three services sit behind every website, and they can sit with three different companies.

- The domain is the address you rent from a registrar, such as yourbusiness.co.uk.
- The hosting is the server that stores the site and sends it to visitors. Squarespace and Wix include it; a self-installed WordPress needs a host.
- The email runs the mailboxes behind hello@yourbusiness.co.uk, and it is a separate service from the website.

DNS records join them up: A and CNAME records point the domain at the website, MX records point it at the mailboxes. {{< a key="domain-hosting-email-guide" >}}Domain vs hosting vs email{{< /a >}} explains how the three fit together, and {{< a key="dns-records-explained" >}}DNS records explained{{< /a >}} goes through each record. **Register the domain in your business's name, with a login you keep**, and switch on automatic renewal.

I have connected domains from Namecheap, Spaceship, Infomaniak, Hostinger, OVH and Google Domains, now part of Squarespace. Squarespace and OVH domains can be fiddly, and the only real snag I have hit was a single dot: the full stop some DNS editors expect at the end of a record's value. My registrars of choice are Spaceship and Namecheap, which built it: fair prices, renewals that do not jump, and support around the clock.

I have used OVH and Hostinger only because clients were already there, and I do not recommend either to a small business owner, as both are a bit complicated to manage. I came across Infomaniak the same way, and that one I do recommend. {{< a key="best-domain-registrar" >}}The best domain registrar in the UK{{< /a >}} compares six I have used, {{< a key="web-hosting-comparison" >}}Hostinger vs OVH vs Infomaniak{{< /a >}} compares the hosts, and {{< a key="professional-email-address" >}}how to get a professional email address{{< /a >}} covers the mailboxes.

## Your small business website launch checklist

Before the site goes live, run through this list. It takes an afternoon, and it saves you months of enquiries lost to a broken form or a page Google cannot see.

### Pages and content

- A home page that says what you do, for whom and where, in its first lines.
- One page per service, each with its own title, description and address.
- An about page with real photos, and a contact page.
- Reviews or examples of your work where visitors will see them.

### Contact

- Your phone number on every page, tappable on a mobile.
- A short form (name, email or phone, message), tested to make sure it reaches you.

### Search and speed

- An SEO title and a description for every page.
- The site added to Google Search Console, with the sitemap submitted: {{< a key="get-website-indexed-google" >}}how to get your website on Google{{< /a >}} shows how.
- Your Google Business Profile linked to the site ({{< a key="google-business-profile-setup" >}}Google Business Profile setup{{< /a >}}).
- Pages that load fast on a phone. Google's "good" thresholds for its Core Web Vitals are 2.5 seconds or less for LCP, 200 milliseconds or less for INP and 0.1 or less for CLS; {{< a key="core-web-vitals-speed" >}}Core Web Vitals explained{{< /a >}} shows how to test and fix them.

### Legal basics in the UK

- A privacy notice that says what you collect through the form and your analytics, and why.
- If you trade as a limited company, your registered name, company number, registered office address and the part of the UK where the company is registered, as the [GOV.UK rules on company disclosures](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material) require.
- A cookie banner for the tools that need consent. The [ICO lists the exceptions](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-the-use-of-storage-and-access-technologies/what-are-the-exceptions/), which since 2026 include some analytics.

## After launch: upkeep, security and enquiries

A website is not finished on launch day. Three habits keep it safe and useful.

### Keep it maintained

On WordPress, the core, theme and plugins need updating every week. On Squarespace, the platform handles the software, and you look after the content, the accounts and the renewals. {{< a key="website-maintenance" >}}Website maintenance{{< /a >}} lists what that covers and what it costs each year.

### Keep it secure

Turn on two-factor authentication for every account, keep backups you have restored at least once, and make sure the domain renews automatically. {{< a key="website-security-guide" >}}Website security for small businesses{{< /a >}} covers prevention and recovery, and if a WordPress site is the problem, here is {{< a key="wordpress-site-hacked" >}}what to do in the first 24 hours after a hack{{< /a >}}.

Eden Flats called me after its site was hacked. That site had never brought in a lead, because the owner had too much on to learn how SEO is done properly. I built a new website, did the SEO and the visual identity, and property owners started calling to have their flats managed: {{< a page="/projects/eden-flats" >}}the Eden Flats project{{< /a >}} shows the site.

### Watch what visitors do

I use Microsoft Clarity, which is free, to see where people engage, what they came for and which part makes them leave: the copy, a photo or the layout. Then I fix that part. Since 31 October 2025, Clarity has needed a consent signal for visitors from the UK, the EEA and Switzerland, so connect it to your cookie banner; {{< a key="microsoft-clarity-tutorial" >}}how to use Microsoft Clarity{{< /a >}} covers the set-up.

## Do it yourself or hand it over?

You do not need me, or anyone like me, if:

- you sell a handful of services and are happy to write about them yourself;
- you will spend a few days learning Squarespace, or you enjoy working with files and an AI assistant;
- you will keep the site up to date, since nobody knows your business better.

Hand it over when:

- prospects judge you on your image, and the site has to make you look established;
- you need custom code, two languages, a shop, or a move between platforms that must keep your Google rankings ({{< a key="wordpress-to-squarespace" >}}moving from WordPress to Squarespace{{< /a >}} shows what that involves);
- the site has been hacked, or a former provider holds the keys.

Whichever route you take, the next steps are the same:

1. Write down the three jobs for your site: who should find you, what should convince your visitors and what they should do next.
2. List your services, one page each.
3. Choose the platform by who will edit it.
4. Gather your texts and photos before anyone builds anything.
5. Budget for the build and for a year of upkeep.
