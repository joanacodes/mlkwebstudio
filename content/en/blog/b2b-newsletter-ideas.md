A good B2B newsletter brings one useful thing per issue, taken from your clients' questions, your projects and what changes in your field. The first issue is easy; the eighth, in a busy month, is the real test.

**A B2B newsletter is a regular email to clients, prospects and business contacts that keeps you in mind until they need what you sell, by being useful rather than by selling.** Below: the formats, how often to send, how long to write, 20 B2B newsletter ideas for service businesses and how to tell whether it works.

Where you publish is a separate choice, covered in my comparison of {{< a key="linkedin-newsletter-vs-email-vs-substack" >}}LinkedIn newsletters, email and Substack{{< /a >}}; the ideas work on all three. This article is part of my {{< a key="funnels-newsletters-guide" >}}complete guide to sales funnels and newsletters{{< /a >}}.

## The formats that work in a B2B newsletter

A format is the shape of an issue. Choose two or three as defaults and rotate the others, so readers know what to expect without getting the same email every month.

| Format | What it looks like | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| One lesson | One idea, one example | Medium |
| A client question answered | A question from a call, answered in full | Low |
| A case study | The brief, what you did, the result | Medium |
| Behind the scenes | How a project really runs | Low |
| A tool review | Who it suits, who it does not, the cost | Medium |
| A regulation update | What changes, for whom, from when | Medium |
| A template | A checklist or a brief to copy | High once |
| Curated links | Three to five links, one line each | Low |
| Monthly numbers | A few figures from your work, explained | Low |

Most issues work best as one main item in one of these formats, plus two or three short ones:

```
Subject: [what the reader gets]
Preview text: [the line that completes it]

Hello [first name],

[Main item: one idea, one example, 200 to 400 words.]

Also this month:
- [Short item, one line, one link]
- [Short item, one line, one link]

[One call to action: a question to reply to, or a booking link.]

[Your name], [your role], [your phone number]
```

Your newsletter tool adds the footer with the unsubscribe link.

## How often to send a B2B newsletter

Consistency matters more than volume. For a small team, **monthly or fortnightly** is the rhythm that lasts: often enough to stay in mind, rarely enough that writing does not eat a day a week.

| Rhythm | Issues a year | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly | 12 | Most service businesses, and anyone writing alone |
| Fortnightly | 26 | Fields that change often, such as tax, law or IT |
| Weekly | 52 | Only if someone has a few hours for it every week |

People are busy. In my cold email campaigns, the third email brings positive replies from people who simply had not found the time to answer the first two. A newsletter relies on the same patience: being there, regularly, on the day the reader has the need and the time.

So choose the rhythm you can keep for a year, not just in January, and state it on the sign-up form ("one email a month"). In a thin month, send one tip and one link rather than nothing. And do not launch a newsletter, a podcast and a LinkedIn routine at once: it is easy to fall for every new channel, and one channel kept up beats three abandoned.

## Length, subject line and one call to action

### Length: a two- or three-minute read

Your readers open it at work, between two tasks. One main item and two or three short ones are plenty; if an issue runs long, split it and you have next month's.

### Subject line and preview text

The subject says what the reader gets, and the preview text (the line shown after it in most inboxes) completes it. Put the useful words first: phones cut long subjects off.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| October newsletter | How long SEO takes for a local business |
| News from [company] | The question every new client asks us |
| Issue 14 | Making Tax Digital: your next update is due 7 November |

Send it from a person: your name and the company in the sender name, and a reply-to address someone reads.

### One call to action

One issue, one thing to do: reply, book a call or download a template. Three buttons split the attention, and you learn nothing from any of them.

In B2B, a reply is the most useful action, because it starts a conversation: end with a question that takes one line to answer. Put your phone number under your name too. In the cold email campaigns I run, many prospects call rather than reply: a call is quicker than writing back, and they like talking to a person.

Sell lightly. One line about what you offer, or about a slot you have free next month, is enough; the rest of the issue is what earns you that line.

## 20 B2B newsletter ideas for service businesses

Each idea is one issue. The examples are illustrations for different trades, not client stories.

### From the questions clients ask

On my first calls, prospects ask whether I have experience, what SEO will bring them and how long it takes to rank. Each of those is an issue, and so are your clients' questions.

1. **Your experience, shown.** One project close to the reader's sector, size and problem says more than a list of logos.
2. **What your service brings, honestly.** What it can do, what it will not, and who should not buy it yet.
3. **How long it really takes.** A realistic timeline, step by step. For SEO, my honest answer is "usually months": I once saw a client's page reach the top of Google within a day or two, and I would still never promise it.
4. **What it costs, and why.** What pushes a price up or down (size, deadline, materials, options), without publishing a price list.
5. **The objection you hear most.** Mine is "cold emailing is illegal". For B2B it is not, within rules that differ between the UK and France; an issue that sets them out with sources, as my {{< a key="b2b-cold-emailing-guide" >}}guide to B2B cold emailing{{< /a >}} does, answers it better than a sales page.

### From your work

6. **A short case study.** The brief, what you did, the result, with the client's permission. My {{< a page="/projects/pilotech" >}}Pilotech project page{{< /a >}} follows exactly that order.
7. **Behind the scenes.** What happens in the week after a client signs: who does what, and what you need from them. It takes the fear out of a first project.
8. **A checklist or template.** "What to prepare before our first meeting", a brief template, a maintenance calendar: something readers copy and keep.
9. **The mistake you fix most often.** What it costs and how to avoid it: an IT support company can tell the story of the backup nobody had ever tested.
10. **Before and after.** A page, a process, a room or a set of accounts: what changed and why, with figures if the client agrees.

### From your field

11. **A regulation update.** What changes, for whom, from when and what to do this month, with a link to the official page. An accountant could remind sole-trader and landlord clients that [Making Tax Digital for Income Tax](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/making-tax-digital-for-income-tax) has applied since 6 April 2026 to qualifying income over £50,000, that the second quarterly update is due by 7 November 2026, and that the threshold falls to £30,000 in April 2027 (as of 19 October 2026).
12. **Curated links.** Three to five things worth reading this month, with one line each on why they matter to your reader. The line is the value, not the link.
13. **A tool review.** One tool you use: who it suits, who it does not, what it costs. An honest verdict is what readers remember.
14. **A myth in your trade.** The belief, then what is true. Mine would be "a website is a one-off cost": it needs maintenance, like any tool you use every day.
15. **A seasonal reminder.** What to do before the year end, the summer or your clients' busy season: an air conditioning installer reminding offices to book a service before the hot months.

### From you and your readers

16. **Monthly numbers.** A few anonymised figures from your work (projects delivered, average response time, the most requested service) and what each means for the reader.
17. **A lesson from something that went wrong.** Anonymous, with what you changed. Mine: before an SEO project starts, I now put in writing how long results usually take, since a client expected them much faster than SEO delivers.
18. **When not to hire you.** The jobs you send elsewhere, and to whom. Telling people honestly whether they need me or someone else is part of how I work; in a newsletter, it shows readers you will tell them the truth.
19. **Your view on a trend.** What AI, a new rule or a new tool changes in your trade, and what it does not. In my work, AI has shortened delivery times, which leaves room to look for better solutions for clients.
20. **A reader's question, answered.** Invite questions ("reply with yours") and answer one per issue, anonymised. It turns the newsletter into a conversation and gives you topics for months.

## Where to find ideas when the page is blank

- **Keep a question log.** Every question a client or prospect asks, on a call or by email, goes into one note. After a month you have more topics than issues.
- **Reuse your blog.** One article becomes one issue: the key point in 150 words and a link to the rest. It works the other way too: an issue that gets replies deserves a full article, written as in my guide to {{< a key="write-seo-blog-posts" >}}blog posts that bring in enquiries{{< /a >}}.
- **Look for complaints.** When I research copy and SEO, I use Claude with deep research to find what people in a niche complain about most. Those complaints make good issues, because your readers have them too.
- **Write in batches.** Three issues drafted in one sitting are easier than one written the night before.

## How to tell whether it works: clicks, replies and calls

Open rates are the figure most tools show first, and the least reliable. Apple Mail's Privacy Protection, offered since iOS 15 in 2021, downloads an email's images, tracking pixel included, in the background whether or not the email is read, so many recorded opens are not opens. My guide to {{< a key="newsletter-metrics" >}}newsletter metrics{{< /a >}} covers what else skews them.

Track these instead, issue by issue:

- **Clicks, link by link**: which topics readers want more of.
- **Replies**: count them. A newsletter that gets replies is working, even with a small list.
- **Calls and enquiries** in the following weeks; ask new prospects how they heard of you.
- **Unsubscribes**: a jump after one issue tells you the topic or the rhythm missed.

A simple sheet is enough: issue, topic, clicks, replies, calls. After six issues, write more of what got replies.

{{< callout type="note" >}}In France, the CNIL's [recommendation on email tracking pixels](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-04/recommandation-pixels_de_suivi.pdf) (April 2026, in French) says a pixel that measures opens for marketing needs the reader's consent, B2B emails included as law firms read it, with narrow exceptions such as security and deliverability. Replies and calls need no tracker. This is a summary, not legal advice: if you write to readers in France and measure opens, have your set-up checked by a lawyer or your DPO.{{< /callout >}}

## Your first three issues: a plan for this month

1. **Set up the welcome emails first**, so a new subscriber hears from you the day they sign up, not three weeks later: see my {{< a key="welcome-email-sequence" >}}welcome email sequence{{< /a >}}.
2. **List the five questions** clients ask most often. They are your first five topics.
3. **Choose your rhythm** and write it on the sign-up form.
4. **Draft three issues in one sitting**: a client question answered, a short case study and a checklist.
5. **Check each issue before sending**: a test email to yourself on a phone and a computer, every link clicked, a reply-to address someone reads, and a list of people who agreed to hear from you or business contacts the rules let you email. My guide to {{< a key="newsletter-consent-gdpr" >}}newsletter consent{{< /a >}} explains the difference.
6. **After three issues**, compare clicks and replies, and keep the formats that worked.

The material is already in your calls and your inbox. Write it in your own voice, keep the rhythm, and let the replies tell you what to write next.
