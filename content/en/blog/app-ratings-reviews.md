Your star rating sits next to your app in App Store search results and on its product page, so people judge it before they read a word of your description. Here is how to get more app reviews on the App Store and Google Play without putting your developer account at risk: ask the right people, at the right moment, through the prompt each store provides, then reply to what they write and fix what they report.

The shortcuts (rewards for ratings, filtering out unhappy users, bought reviews) break Apple's and Google's rules, and in the UK and the EU they also break consumer law. Everything below reflects the stores' documentation as of 19 October 2026.

For where ratings fit into a release as a whole, see my {{< a key="mobile-app-launch-guide" >}}complete guide to launching a mobile app{{< /a >}}.

## Why ratings matter for downloads and ranking

Apple shows a summary rating on your product page and in search results, and says ratings and reviews "can influence how your app ranks in App Store search". A weak score costs you twice: fewer people find the app, and fewer of those who find it install it.

Google Play does not publish how much ratings weigh in its search. It does say its public rating is weighted towards recent ratings, to reflect the changes you make, and that poor technical quality (crash and freeze rates above its Android vitals thresholds) can reduce your app's visibility. A buggy release hurts your stars and your visibility at the same time.

The words matter as much as the stars. As of 19 October 2026, the App Store shows a short summary of your reviews, generated by large language models, in the US and eight other English-language storefronts, the UK among them. What many reviewers repeat is what that paragraph is likely to say. My {{< a key="aso-guide" >}}beginner's guide to ASO{{< /a >}} covers the other ranking factors, and my {{< a key="app-store-listing" >}}guide to App Store and Google Play listings{{< /a >}} covers the name, subtitle and description that sit next to your rating.

## How to get more app reviews: use the official prompts

Both stores provide a rating window that opens inside your app, so people can rate without leaving it. Use it and nothing else: Apple's guideline 5.6.1 tells developers to use the provided API and says Apple "will disallow custom review prompts".

### On iOS: the StoreKit review request

The current API is `RequestReviewAction` (the `requestReview` environment value in SwiftUI); the older `SKStoreReviewController` has been deprecated since iOS 18. Your app asks, and the system decides whether to show anything:

- If the person has not rated the app on that device, the request appears at most three times in a 365-day period.
- If they have, it only appears again for a new version, once more than 365 days have passed since their last review.
- People can switch these requests off for every app on their device.

Because the call may show nothing, Apple says not to make it in response to a button tap. For a permanent "Rate this app" line in your settings, link to your App Store page with `action=write-review` added to the URL: it opens the page where people write a review.

### On Android: the Play In-App Review API

Google's equivalent shows a card with one to five stars and an optional comment. Google applies a time-bound quota it does not disclose: call the flow more than once in a short period (its example is less than a month) and the card may not appear. Show the card exactly as it is, with nothing on top of it, and never from a button; for a settings link, send people to your Play Store page instead.

Neither API tells your app whether someone left a rating, and Google's documentation adds that its API does not even say whether the card was shown. You cannot reward a review you cannot see, which is just as well, as the next sections show.

## When to ask: right after a success moment

Timing does most of the work. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines say to ask only once people have shown engagement, for example after completing a level or a significant task, and to avoid first launch and onboarding, when people may be more likely to leave negative feedback. Google says to wait until the person has used enough of the app to give useful feedback.

| Good moments to ask | Moments to avoid |
|---|---|
| A booking or an order is confirmed | First launch and onboarding |
| A job or a delivery is marked as done | The middle of a task or a game level |
| The third or fourth successful use of the main feature | Right after an error, a crash or a failed payment |
| A goal or a streak is reached | A tap on a "Rate us" button (link to the store instead) |

Space the requests out: Apple suggests at least a week or two between them, after more engagement. Apple's own sample code waits until someone has completed a task four times, has not yet been asked about the current version and has paused on the success screen for a few seconds. Apple calls the number four arbitrary, so choose what fits how often people use your app.

If your app has a few hundred regular users rather than millions, every chance to ask counts. Put the request on the success moment that everybody reaches, not on a feature only your keenest users find.

## What both stores forbid: rewards, review gating and pressure

Three practices are off limits on both stores.

### Rewards for ratings

Apple's [App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) warn that developers who try to inflate their rankings with "paid, incentivized, filtered, or fake feedback" may be expelled from the Apple Developer Program. Google Play's policy on user ratings, reviews and installs bans inflating ratings or reviews by illegitimate means such as incentives, and gives the example of a notification offering a discount for a high rating. So no coins, extra lives, discount codes or prize draws in exchange for a review.

### Review gating

Review gating means asking people whether they like your app first, then sending only the happy ones to the store. Google's [in-app review guidelines](https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/in-app-review) say your app shouldn't ask any question before or while showing the rating card, and give "Do you like the app?" as an example. On Apple's side, "filtered" feedback is in the list quoted above.

### Pressure and look-alike prompts

Guideline 3.2.2(x) says apps must not force people to rate or review the app to use it or reach its content. A screen that stays blocked until someone rates is out, and so is a home-made pop-up that imitates the system prompt (guideline 5.6.1 again).

What you can do is make your support contact easy to find, in the app and on your store page. Apple recommends it because it gives unhappy users a way to reach you before they reach the review form. The difference with gating: support is there for everyone, all the time, rather than placed as a filter in front of the rating prompt.

## Fake and incentivised reviews also break UK and EU law

In the UK, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has banned fake reviews since 6 April 2025. Schedule 20, paragraph 13 covers submitting or commissioning a fake review or a review that hides an incentive, and offering services that help others do it. The CMA's guidance also treats hiding negative reviews or cherry-picking positive ones as misleading, and the CMA can now fine businesses directly, up to 10% of global turnover. Its [fake reviews guidance (CMA208)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews-cma208) includes a short guide for businesses that publish reviews.

In the EU, the Omnibus Directive (EU) 2019/2161 added two practices to the list that the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive bans in all circumstances (Annex I, points 23b and 23c): claiming reviews come from real users without reasonable and proportionate checks, and submitting, commissioning or misrepresenting reviews. It also added Article 7(6), which treats whether and how you check the reviews you publish as essential information for consumers, something to keep in mind if you show reviews on {{< a key="app-website-seo" >}}your app's website{{< /a >}}. These rules have applied since 28 May 2022, and if you have users in the EU, treat them as yours wherever your company is based. The text is on [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/2161/oj).

This is general information, not legal advice: if you plan anything beyond the store prompts, such as a tester programme, a referral scheme or an agency that promises reviews, check it with a consumer-law solicitor first.

## Reply to reviews on both stores

A reply does not change the stars, but on both stores the reviewer is notified and can update their review. Everyone else reads your reply too, so write it for future users as much as for the reviewer.

### In App Store Connect

Open **Apps**, choose your app, click **Ratings and Reviews** in the sidebar, pick the platform at the top right, then click **Reply** next to the review. You need the Account Holder, Admin or Customer Support role. Replies can take up to 24 hours to appear, one reply per review is shown, and you can edit or delete it at any time.

### In Play Console

Open your app, go to **Ratings and reviews > Reviews**, type your reply and select **Publish reply**. You need the "Reply to reviews" permission. Each review gets one public reply, the user receives a push notification and an email, and you can edit the reply later.

### How I would write them

1. Start with the lowest ratings and the reviews about bugs in the current version: Apple suggests that order when you cannot answer everything.
2. Thank the person, name the exact problem and say what you are doing about it.
3. Point to your support address, never to the reviewer's personal details: guideline 5.6.1 rules out personal information, spam and marketing in replies.
4. Do not ask for a better rating. Fix the problem and let the notification do the asking.
5. On iOS, send download and billing problems to Apple Support, as Apple asks developers to do.

```text
Thanks for reporting this, and sorry about the lost booking. The sync problem
on Android 15 is fixed in version 2.4, out today. If it happens again, write to
support@yourapp.com with the time of the booking and we will look into it.
```

## Turn bad reviews into fixes, then decide whether to reset

Low ratings are bug reports with an audience. Read them version by version: App Store Connect filters reviews by version and by rating, and Play Console breaks ratings down by app version and device type. When the same complaint comes back, fix it, mention the fix in your release notes, then reply to the reviews that raised it. Apple recommends this to win back unhappy users, and the notification gives them a reason to update their review.

Google Play weights its public rating towards recent ratings, so a fixed version shows in the score as new ratings arrive. On the App Store, you have one more option: resetting the overview rating when you release a new version.

To do it, select the new version in the App Store Connect sidebar, select **Reset rating when this version is released** in the **Reset Overview Rating** section, then save. The reset applies to every country at once, your written reviews stay on the page, and once the version is out you cannot restore the old score. Until enough people rate the new version, your page says the rating was recently reset.

Apple recommends using this sparingly, because a page with few ratings can put people off. I would only reset when the old score comes from a problem the new version really fixes, and when you have enough active users to rebuild a score quickly.

## Checklist before your next release

1. The review request is tied to one or two success moments, never to launch, onboarding, an error or a button.
2. Nothing sits in front of the system prompt: no question, no reward, no home-made pop-up.
3. Your settings screen has a "Rate this app" link that opens the store page (`action=write-review` on iOS).
4. Your support contact is easy to find in the app and on both store pages.
5. Someone reads and answers new reviews every week, lowest ratings first.
6. Your release notes name the fixes reviewers asked for, and those reviewers get a reply.
7. If you buy installs with {{< a key="apple-ads-tutorial" >}}Apple Ads{{< /a >}}, your rating is in good shape first: the ads send people to a product page where they see it before they install.
