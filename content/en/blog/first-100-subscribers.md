Your first 100 newsletter subscribers will not be strangers. They will be clients, suppliers, people you met at an event and people who read your LinkedIn posts. So learning how to get newsletter subscribers from zero is less about growth tricks than about asking the people who already know your name, in the places where they already see it.

This guide is for small teams starting from nothing. It covers those places in the order I would use them, explains why a bought list is a false shortcut, and shows how to record where each subscriber came from, so that after three months you know which channels deserve your time. It assumes your newsletter tool is ready, with a sign-up form, a confirmation email (double opt-in) and a welcome email. For where a newsletter fits in the rest of your marketing, start with {{< a key="funnels-newsletters-guide" >}}my guide to sales funnels and newsletters{{< /a >}}.

## Why buying a list costs more than it saves

A ready-made file of thousands of "opted-in" addresses looks like a shortcut. Four problems make it a bad one.

### The consent was given to someone else

In the UK, marketing emails to individuals, sole traders included, need their consent or the soft opt-in under regulation 22 of PECR, as the [ICO's guidance on email marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-electronic-mail-marketing-rules/) explains. The ICO's position on bought lists is that consent only travels with a list if your organisation was named when people gave it: "selected third parties" does not count, and checking is your job as the sender. In France, the CNIL also expects the full list of partners to be available at the moment people consent. Companies fall outside PECR's consent rule, but the next three problems apply to them as well.

### Your email tool forbids it

Mailchimp's [Acceptable Use Policy](https://mailchimp.com/en-gb/legal/acceptable_use/) bans purchased, rented and third-party lists, partner lists and addresses gathered from public sources. Read your own tool's terms before importing any address you did not collect yourself: an account closed over a bought list takes your genuine subscribers with it.

### Spam traps

Some addresses exist only to catch senders who never asked for permission: addresses that never signed up for anything, or old ones that a provider closed and later reused. Bought lists are where they end up, and hitting them can put your domain on a blocklist that follows you from one tool to the next. A verifier such as Reoon, which I use every day, flags some of them, but a list that needs cleaning for traps is a list you should not mail.

### Complaints

People who never asked for your emails click "Report spam". Google's [email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126) ask senders to keep their spam rate below 0.1% and never to reach 0.3%: on a mailing of 1,000 emails, three complaints make 0.3%. Past that point your emails are more likely to land in spam, including for the clients who wanted them. The Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook rules are explained in {{< a key="newsletter-deliverability" >}}my guide to newsletter deliverability{{< /a >}}.

Consent, the soft opt-in and double opt-in are covered in {{< a key="newsletter-consent-gdpr" >}}my guide to newsletter consent{{< /a >}}.{{< /callout >}}

## How to get newsletter subscribers from people who already know you

Start with a list on paper, not in your email tool. Write down everyone who knows your work: current and past clients, suppliers, partners, people you met at events, people who email you questions, your LinkedIn connections. That number is the honest starting point of your newsletter.

Then **invite them; do not import them**. A short personal message with a link to your sign-up page lets each person subscribe, confirm with one click and arrive with a consent record your tool keeps. It also means your first readers chose you, which is what makes the first issues worth writing.

Who can receive that invitation by email depends on who they are:

- People at companies: PECR's consent rule does not cover companies, and in France the CNIL accepts emails to professionals about their line of work, provided they were told and can object easily. A one-to-one invitation is fine.
- Clients who are individuals: the soft opt-in lets you email people whose details you collected during a sale, or negotiations for one, about your own similar services, but only if you offered a way to refuse at that moment and in every message since. In France, the equivalent exception (article L34-5 of the Code des postes et des communications électroniques) needs an actual sale or service.
- Everyone else: ask in person, on a call or at the end of a project. In the UK, an email asking people whether they want your marketing is itself marketing; the ICO fined Flybe and Honda for exactly that in 2017.

Last, add one line to your email signature, under your name: what the newsletter is, how often it arrives, and the link. For example: "One practical tip on [your subject] every month: subscribe here." Every email you already send becomes a quiet invitation.

## Make your website ask in four places

Your website is where strangers turn into readers. Four places do most of the work:

1. The end of each article. Someone who has just finished an article on your subject is the person most likely to want the next one. One field, one button, one sentence on what they will receive.
2. A dedicated sign-up page, at an address such as /newsletter. Say what the newsletter covers, how often it arrives and that unsubscribing takes one click, and show a past issue. This page is the link you share everywhere else in this guide.
3. The footer. A short form on every page catches the visitors who never open an article.
4. A pop-up, sparingly. Show it after a visitor has scrolled most of a page or is about to leave, and only once. Never open with a screen that hides the content on a phone: Google's search guidance treats intrusive interstitials as a poor page experience.

A lead magnet, such as a checklist or a template that solves one small problem next to what you sell, gives people a reason to sign up today rather than someday. Deliver it to everyone who asks and offer the newsletter as a separate, unticked box; {{< a key="lead-magnet-ideas" >}}my 12 lead magnet ideas{{< /a >}} show what works for service businesses.

If adding a form to your articles means fighting your theme, fix that before promoting the newsletter anywhere else. It is usually a small job for whoever looks after your {{< a page="/services/websites" >}}website{{< /a >}}, and a site that loses the visitors you send it wastes every other effort in this guide.

## LinkedIn: your posts and the Featured section

If your clients are businesses, many of them already read you on LinkedIn. Two habits turn those readers into subscribers.

First, turn each issue into a post. Take one idea from the issue, write it so it stands on its own, and end with one line saying the full version goes out by email, with the link to your sign-up page.

Second, use the **Featured** section of your profile. It sits near the top, under About, and can hold a link: point it to your sign-up page, with a title that says what readers get.

Do not send the same invitation to every connection by private message: it reads as a mass mailing because it is one. LinkedIn also has its own newsletter format, a different choice with different trade-offs, which I compare in {{< a key="linkedin-newsletter-vs-email-vs-substack" >}}LinkedIn newsletter, email newsletter or Substack{{< /a >}}.

## Borrow other people's audiences

Once the people you know have had their invitation, the next subscribers come from audiences that belong to someone else, with that person's agreement.

- Talks, workshops and trade fairs: put a QR code to your sign-up page on your last slide or on your stand, with one line on what people get. A business card handed to you is not a sign-up, so ask, or let them scan the code.
- Partners and newsletter swaps: find a business that serves the same clients without competing with you, as an accountant and an employment lawyer do, and agree to recommend each other's newsletter in one issue. **You swap mentions, never addresses.**
- Guest articles and podcasts: write for a trade publication or a partner's blog, or appear on a podcast your clients listen to, and ask for the link in your bio to point to your sign-up page rather than your home page.

## Referral programmes: when readers bring readers

A referral programme gives every subscriber a personal link; when friends sign up through it, the subscriber earns a reward, such as a template, a guide or a mention in an issue. Some tools build it in. At the time of writing (October 2026), beehiiv includes one from its paid Scale plan, not on the free Launch plan, and Kit offers a newsletter referral system on its Pro plan, run with SparkLoop, according to [Kit's plan comparison](https://help.kit.com/en/articles/4494683-creator-vs-pro). For other tools, look at their integrations; {{< a key="newsletter-tools-comparison" >}}my comparison of newsletter tools{{< /a >}} covers what each plan includes.

Two rules keep it clean. The friend must sign up through the link: never use a form where subscribers type a friend's address for your tool to email, because that friend never asked. And keep rewards modest, or you will attract people who want the reward rather than your emails.

A referral programme multiplies an audience you already have. With 30 subscribers there is little to multiply, so set it up once readers clearly like what they receive.

## Tag every sign-up with its source

In three months you will want to know which channels brought readers who open, reply and stay. Your tool can tell you, if you set it up from the first subscriber:

- One form per place (end of articles, footer, sign-up page, pop-up), each adding its own tag. Kit and Mailchimp call them tags, MailerLite calls them groups and Brevo uses lists.
- One link per channel for everything outside your website: the signature, LinkedIn, a partner's issue, podcast notes. UTM parameters on each link let your analytics show where visitors to the sign-up page came from.
- A record of consent for each subscriber: the date, the form and the wording they saw. Keep a copy of old forms when you change them.

Every quarter, keep the two or three channels that bring readers who stay and drop the rest. If I had to insist on one thing about marketing, it would be this: it is easy to fall for the shiny new channel, and far more useful to focus on the ones you already use.

## What to expect from your first 100

No honest benchmark tells you how long 100 subscribers take, because it depends on how many people already know you and how many visit your website. Your own numbers give a better estimate, and this table shows where each channel tends to sit:

| Channel | Effort | When sign-ups arrive |
|---|---|---|
| Personal invitations | Low, once | Within days, then it stops |
| Email signature | Very low, once | Slowly, as you send emails |
| Website forms | Low, once | Steadily, in line with your traffic |
| LinkedIn posts and Featured | Medium, each issue | Around each post |
| Talks and events | High, each event | On the day |
| Partner swaps and guest spots | Medium, each time | When the issue or episode comes out |
| Referral programme | A paid plan and some setup | Once readers like the issues |

Expect a first wave from the people you invite, then a slower, steadier flow from your website and partners. That is normal, not a sign the newsletter is failing. What makes the second hundred easier is what the first hundred receive: a {{< a key="welcome-email-sequence" >}}welcome sequence{{< /a >}} that introduces you and your best material, and issues that arrive when you said they would. Some people will unsubscribe; a reader who leaves costs you nothing and keeps your figures honest.

## Your first-100 checklist

1. Set up your tool with a sign-up page, double opt-in and a welcome email.
2. List the people who know your work and invite them one by one: by email where the rules allow it, in person otherwise.
3. Add the newsletter line to your email signature.
4. Add forms at the end of your articles and in your footer, each with its own tag.
5. Put the sign-up page in your LinkedIn Featured section and turn each issue into a post.
6. Plan one talk, swap or guest appearance a month.
7. Send every issue on schedule, from the first subscriber.
8. After three months, compare your sources and keep what works.
9. Add a referral programme once your issues get replies.
