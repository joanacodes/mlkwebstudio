On a first call, new clients ask me three questions: "Do you have experience?", "What is SEO and how will it benefit me?" and "How long does it take to rank?". The third deserves a straighter answer than "it depends", so here is how long SEO takes. Google usually indexes a new page within days, the first rankings appear within weeks, and a steady flow of enquiries from search takes four months to a year in most cases. That last range is Google's, not mine.

Those are three different clocks, and mixing them up is how business owners give up on SEO a few months before it starts to pay. Below, I separate them, show you the timeline of one of my clients (unusually fast, so read the warning that comes with it), then explain what speeds SEO up, how to see progress before the phone rings and what to do while you wait.

If you are starting from scratch, my {{< a key="small-business-seo-guide" >}}complete guide to small business SEO{{< /a >}} covers the groundwork this timeline assumes.

## Three clocks: indexing, rankings and enquiries

"SEO results" can mean any of these three things:

| Clock | What it means | Usual time |
|---|---|---|
| Indexing | Google has found your page and stored it, so it can appear in results | Days, sometimes a few weeks |
| Rankings | Your page appears for searches, first far down the results, then higher | Weeks to months |
| Enquiries | People click, then call you or fill in your form | Four months to a year, in most cases |

Indexing is the fast clock, as long as nothing blocks it. Google's page on [asking Google to recrawl your URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/ask-google-to-recrawl) says crawling can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks, and that asking for a crawl does not guarantee the page will be included quickly, or even at all. More recent figures came from Google's Gary Illyes at a Search Central event in Barcelona in early October 2026. According to [recaps of the talk](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-crawling-indexing-serving-data-42225.html) (Google has not published the slides), a new URL is typically discovered in about 20 hours, while the slowest cases take weeks or months, and some pages are never indexed, often for quality reasons. If a page is still missing after a few weeks, my tutorial on {{< a key="get-website-indexed-google" >}}getting a new website on Google{{< /a >}} goes through the checks.

Rankings come next. A new page usually starts far down the results, for long and specific searches, then moves up over the following weeks and months if it answers those searches better than the pages already there. The first sign is not traffic but impressions: Search Console counts one each time your page shows up in someone's results, even on page five.

Enquiries are the slow clock, and the only one that pays the bills. Google's own guidance on [hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) says that, in most cases, it takes four months to a year to put improvements in place and then see the potential benefit. The first page is also harder to reach than it looks: when Ahrefs [followed a random sample of newly published pages](https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-rank/), only 1.74% reached Google's top 10 within a year. Counting only English pages with real content, the figure was about 6%, the one Ahrefs considers fairer. Either way, a top-10 position within a year is the exception, not the rule.

## How long does SEO take for a new website?

For a new site, the three clocks run one after the other. This is the order I would expect, with what to look at in Google Search Console at each stage. Treat the timings as rough guides, not a schedule: a narrow local offer can go through these stages in weeks, a broad national one can take years.

| Stage | What usually happens | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| The first days | Google discovers and indexes your pages, if nothing blocks them | URL Inspection, page by page |
| The first weeks | Impressions appear, mostly for long, specific searches, often several pages down | Impressions and average position |
| The first months | Pages that match a search well start to climb; first clicks, sometimes a first enquiry | Clicks per page and the queries behind them |
| Four months to a year | Google's range for real benefit: regular clicks and enquiries on your best pages | Enquiries you can trace back to search |
| After a year | Gains build on each other: pages collect links and mentions, and each new page joins a site Google already knows | Comparison with the same months last year |

An existing business that moves to a new website does not start from zero, as long as every old address redirects to its new one, but its positions can wobble for a while: according to the same Barcelona recaps, a site move typically takes one to three months to settle. If you are changing platform or domain, plan the switch outside your busiest season.

## A real example: a first lead three weeks after launch

Pilotech installs air conditioning, heat pumps and ventilation in Paris and the Île-de-France. I have worked with them for two years, on their websites, their SEO and their cold emailing, and their timeline was unusually fast:

- Building the website took about a month.
- The first lead arrived three weeks after it went live.
- A top ranking for one of the searches it targets came within a day or two. That is rare: it normally takes far longer.

The part I learnt the most from is what did not work. Of the five websites I have built for this client, one did not work at all: it offered too many services and was too broad. The one that ranks, {{< a page="/projects/pilotech-teknopoint" >}}the Teknopoint site{{< /a >}}, sells one specific thing: air conditioning with no outdoor unit, for Paris buildings where a unit on the façade is not allowed. It ranks first or second on Google. Same client, same studio: the specific site ranks, the broad one never worked.

## What makes SEO faster, or slower

The same work can take a few weeks for one business and years for another. Here is what usually makes the difference.

| Faster when | Slower when |
|---|---|
| You sell one specific thing, and each page targets one search | One site tries to rank for every service you offer |
| Few businesses compete for your searches, often local ones | You compete nationally with big brands, directories and media sites |
| Pages use the words your customers type into Google | Pages talk about "solutions" and "excellence" without saying what you do |
| Google can crawl the site easily and it loads fast on a phone | Pages are blocked, duplicated or slow |
| Other sites mention you: local press, partners, suppliers | Nobody outside your own site mentions you |
| You add and improve pages steadily, month after month | You publish ten articles in a week, then nothing for a year |

The first row is the Pilotech lesson. A specific offer gives Google, and the customer, a clear answer to one search; a site that sells everything competes on every search at once and often wins none. Local searches usually move faster too, because fewer businesses compete for them. For local results, Google says it ranks businesses on relevance, distance and prominence, and that nobody can pay for a better local position.

### What you do not control

Google updates its core ranking systems a few times a year: so far in 2026, core updates rolled out in March and May. A core update can move your positions in either direction without you changing anything. According to the Barcelona recaps, recovering from a drop after a core update typically takes three to six months. That is one more reason to judge SEO over quarters, not weeks.

## How to tell if SEO is working before the phone rings

Google Search Console is free, and it shows progress months before the enquiries arrive. If you have not set it up yet, my {{< a key="google-search-console-tutorial" >}}Google Search Console tutorial{{< /a >}} takes you through it. Then check these, in this order:

1. **Indexing.** Are the pages you care about indexed? The URL Inspection tool answers for one page, the page indexing report for the whole site.
2. **Impressions.** Rising impressions are the first real sign, even while your pages are still far down.
3. **Average position** for the searches you target. Watch the trend over several weeks, not the daily figure.
4. **Clicks**, page by page, and the queries behind them.
5. **Enquiries.** Forms and calls. Ask every new customer how they found you, and write the answer down.

Since 31 August 2026, every site also has a generative AI report in Search Console, which shows how often your pages appear in AI Overviews and AI Mode. It counts impressions only, with no clicks or queries, but it tells you whether Google's AI answers show your pages at all.

When I take on SEO for a client, I work on it for one to three months, then we discuss what comes next: another plan, or we stop there. In my experience, SEO for a clear, specific offer works in the end, because it simply takes time; the figures above tell you whether it is heading the right way.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}On the day you start, add an annotation to the **Performance** chart in Search Console (custom annotations arrived in November 2025). Every later review then shows exactly where the work began, and the **Compare** option in the date filter puts the periods before and after side by side.{{< /callout >}}

## When SEO is too slow: what to do while you wait

If you need enquiries this month, SEO alone will not bring them. Do not stop it: pair it with a channel that works faster.

- **Google Ads** can put you above the organic results within a day or two, for as long as you pay. It is not that tricky, but it rewards a little research into what your customers search. I also use ad data the other way round: what advertisers pay for a search tells me which searches are worth winning with SEO. My comparison of {{< a key="seo-vs-ppc" >}}SEO vs PPC{{< /a >}} explains when each one makes sense.
- **A Google Business Profile** can show you in Maps and local results without waiting for your website to rank. It is free, and it is the base of {{< a key="local-seo-guide" >}}local SEO{{< /a >}}.
- **Cold emailing**, if you sell to other businesses. On the campaigns I run, the first result comes within the first 36 hours. My {{< a key="b2b-cold-emailing-guide" >}}guide to B2B cold emailing{{< /a >}} shows how to start.

One warning from experience: waiting makes it tempting to try every new tool and channel at once. There is a lot of noise in marketing, that is rather the point of it, and small teams fall into the shiny-object trap easily. Pick one fast channel, keep SEO running and judge each one on its own clock.

## Before you start: put the expectations in writing

A client once refused to pay me after six months of SEO, because the results they expected had not arrived. Six months is well inside the four-months-to-a-year range Google gives. The lesson I took from it: agree on the expectations in writing before any work starts. That applies when you hire someone, and it applies when you do the work yourself.

Write down, on one page:

1. **The searches you want to appear for**, in your customers' words, with one main search per page.
2. **Your starting point**: impressions, clicks and average position over the last three months, from the Performance report.
3. **What you will check, and when**: for example indexing after the first month, impressions and positions after three, clicks and enquiries from the fourth month on.
4. **The horizon**: four months to a year for steady enquiries, longer for competitive national searches.
5. **What happens at each review**: carry on, change the plan or stop.
6. **The faster channel** you will run in the meantime, if you need enquiries before then.

With that page agreed, month six becomes a review rather than an argument.
