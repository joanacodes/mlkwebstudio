An SEO blog post is an article that answers one question your future customers type into Google. It is written to rank for that search, then to lead the reader to the page where they can call you or ask for a quote. Most small business blogs manage the first half at best: a few visits, no enquiries. This guide shows how to write SEO blog posts that do both.

It is the method I use on my clients' websites. Once a client has approved the site, I write the metadata and prepare a few SEO articles in advance, so the blog does not start empty. The topics come from what customers ask and complain about, and each article points to a page that sells.

The eight steps below, and the checks after publishing, are part of my {{< a key="small-business-seo-guide" >}}small business SEO guide{{< /a >}}.

## What makes a blog post bring in enquiries

A blog post that brings in work does two jobs. It ranks for a search made by someone with a problem, and it takes that person, in one click, to the page that solves it.

My clearest example is the {{< a page="/projects/pilotech-vmi" >}}site I built for Pilotech's ventilation work{{< /a >}}. Its blog has 21 articles, each built around a concrete problem, such as condensation in attics, black mould in a flat or a damp wine cellar. **Every article links to the page of the unit suited to the problem it describes.** A visitor reads the article, moves on to the unit, downloads its brochure, then calls or emails for a quote.

| Rarely brings enquiries | Brings enquiries |
|---|---|
| Company news: a new van, a trade fair | A problem in the customer's words: "condensation on bedroom walls" |
| Generic tips anyone could have written | A question asked before buying: "can I have air conditioning without an outdoor unit?" |
| A definition rewritten from other sites | A comparison of the options, saying who each one suits |

News posts have their place for people who already know you. They rarely rank, because nobody searches for them.

## Step 1: find topics in your customers' problems

The best topics are the questions people ask before they need you, or while they hesitate. You already hear most of them.

- Your calls and emails: on a first call, my clients ask whether I have experience, what SEO is and how it will benefit them, and how long it takes to rank. Each of those deserves an article, like any question you answer every week.
- Customer complaints: I use Deep Research in Claude to find what customers complain about most in a niche, and which words and questions keep coming back. The prompts are in {{< a key="deep-research-copywriting-seo" >}}how to use Deep Research for copywriting and SEO{{< /a >}}.
- Google's suggestions: type the start of a question into Google and read the predictions, which Google says "reflect real searches that have been done on Google". The **People also ask** box adds related questions.
- Search Console: once your site is live, it shows the searches you already appear for. In **Performance** > **Search results**, add a filter on **Query**, choose **Custom (regex)** and paste this pattern to list only the questions:

```text
^(how|what|why|when|where|which|who|can|do|does|is|are|should)\b
```

Prefer narrow topics to broad ones. Of the websites I built for one client, the one that offered too many different services did not work at all, while the one built around very specific services ranked. Articles behave the same way: "air conditioning" is a crowded subject, but "air conditioning in a listed building" is a question a local installer can win.

## Step 2: give each article one keyword people search for

Once you have a question, check the words people use to ask it, and how many do. Google Trends compares phrasings, Keyword Planner gives volume ranges and Semrush adds what advertisers pay per click. I use Semrush mainly for that last figure: the keywords advertisers pay for show which searches bring business, so I double down on them in SEO instead of paying for every click. The method is in my guide to {{< a key="keyword-research-small-business" >}}keyword research for small businesses{{< /a >}}.

Three rules keep a blog in order:

1. **One main keyword per article**, with the close variants that come with it.
2. **One article per keyword.** Two articles on the same search compete with each other, and Google has to guess which one to show. Search your own site before you start writing.
3. **Your customers' words, not your trade's.** For Pilotech's Teknopoint pages, people searched for air conditioning "without an outdoor unit" or for "invisible air conditioning", not for the names of the ranges.

Then search the keyword yourself. If the first results are all local service pages, people want a provider: write a service page instead. If they are guides and comparisons, a blog post fits.

## Step 3: answer the question in the first lines

The reader typed a question, so give the answer in the first two or three sentences, then the detail. That is why every article on this blog opens with a short answer.

My rule for cold emails is the opposite: the first email is three lines at most, because people hate long emails they never asked for. A blog reader did ask. The article can be long, as long as the answer comes first. Here is an opening, before and after:

```text
Before:
Damp is a problem many homeowners face. In this article, we look
at the causes of condensation and the solutions available. Read
on to find out more.

After:
Condensation forms when warm, damp air meets a cold window or wall.
To reduce it, air each room for a few minutes every day, dry washing
outside when you can and run the extractor fan when you cook or
shower. If the windows still stream every morning, it is time to
look at ventilation that runs all the time.
```

The first lines matter for AI search too: Google now answers many simple questions with an AI Overview at the top of its results, and a plainly stated answer is easier to pick up. Google's [guide to its generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide), published in May 2026, says optimising for them "is still SEO", with no need to chunk or rewrite content for AI. More in {{< a key="write-content-for-ai-search" >}}how to write content that AI assistants actually quote{{< /a >}}.

## Step 4: build the outline from the reader's next questions

Write the outline before the article. After the first answer, the reader has other questions: why it happens, what they can do themselves, what the options are, what they cost, which mistakes to avoid, when to call a professional. Give each one an H2, so that someone skimming the headings understands the answer.

For the condensation article, the outline could be:

```text
Title: How to stop condensation in a flat
Short answer: two or three sentences
H2 Why condensation forms
H2 What you can do yourself, room by room
H2 When airing is not enough: ventilation options compared
H2 What each option costs to buy and to run
H2 Mistakes that make it worse
H2 When to call a professional (link to the service page)
FAQ: three to five short questions
```

Inside each section, use the format that answers fastest: numbered steps for a procedure, a table for a comparison, a short list for options. Keep paragraphs to two to four sentences, and use photos of your own work rather than stock images.

## Step 5: add what only your business knows

A post that repeats the first page of Google gives Google no reason to rank it above the pages it repeats. What earns the ranking is what only you can write:

- the problems you see on jobs, and what caused them;
- how long the work takes, and what makes the price go up or down;
- honest advice, including when the reader does not need you.

I tell clients when they should call me and when they should call someone else, and I write articles the same way: readers trust a post that says who a solution does not suit.

Google's guidance on [helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) looks for the same things: original information or analysis, value beyond the sources a page draws on, and a clear author. Before I write for a client, I ask about the company, what it offers and which clients it prefers working with: material no competitor has.

## Step 6: draft with AI, then rework it by hand

I write with AI, and since it arrived my delivery times have shrunk. I give the assistant the brief, the questions, the keyword and the facts, then go round, drafting and editing, until I am satisfied with the text. I use Claude: in my experience, ChatGPT tends to hallucinate a bit too much, while Claude is extremely accurate.

The rework is where the article is made:

1. Ask for one section at a time, not the whole article.
2. Where a fact is missing, have the assistant write [TO CONFIRM] rather than guess.
3. Check every figure, name and date against a source or with the client.
4. Read the draft aloud and cut whatever you would never say to a customer.

Google accepts AI-assisted content that helps people, but mass-producing pages that add no value can break its spam policy on scaled content abuse. Since October 2026, its [guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) also calls it critical to fact-check and review AI-generated content by hand before publishing. My editing method is in {{< a key="ai-writing-sound-human" >}}how to make AI writing sound human{{< /a >}}.

## Step 7: link the article to the page that solves the problem

An article that ranks but leads nowhere brings visits, not enquiries. Link it to the service or product page that solves the problem, in the sentence where the reader is most convinced, with words that say where the link goes: "our ventilation units for flats" rather than "click here".

Link back from the service page to the articles that answer its common questions, and link related articles to each other and to the main guide on their subject.

Then make the next step easy. Many prospects prefer to call: in the cold email campaigns I run for Pilotech, architects especially often called instead of replying, because a call fits a busy day. So the page an article leads to should show a phone number, not only a form. On the {{< a page="/projects/pilotech-teknopoint" >}}Teknopoint site{{< /a >}}, the blog answers the questions people ask before buying, such as how to hide an air conditioner, and the range pages give the next step.

## Step 8: write the title, meta description and URL

Google's [Search Essentials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials) ask you to use the words people would use to look for your content, in prominent places such as the title and main heading, and in descriptive places such as alt text and link text. For a blog post, that gives:

| Element | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| SEO title | The keyword first, then the promise, in about 60 characters | How to stop condensation in a flat: what works |
| H1 | The title, or close to it | How to stop condensation in a flat |
| URL | The keyword, in a few words | /blog/stop-condensation-flat |
| Meta description | What the reader will learn, in about 150 characters | Why condensation forms in a flat, the everyday habits that reduce it, and which ventilation to choose when opening the windows is not enough. |
| Image alt text | What the photo shows | Condensation on a bedroom window in winter |

Google decides what appears in its results. It usually starts from your title tag, but may use your main heading or other text when the title is vague or stuffed with keywords, and it shows your meta description when that describes the page better than the page's own text. Write both for a person choosing between ten results.

I do the metadata once the content is final, and it pays: a client's site I built on Squarespace, a closed platform, ranks first or second on Google with the right keywords and metadata. Every setting is in my {{< a key="on-page-seo-checklist" >}}on-page SEO checklist{{< /a >}}.

## After publishing: measure enquiries, then update

Search Console tells you what each article ranks for. In **Performance** > **Search results**, open the **Pages** tab, click the article, then open **Queries**. Three patterns tell you what to do next:

- Many impressions, few clicks: the title or the description does not match the search. Rewrite them.
- An average position just off page one: the article is close. Add the sub-question it misses, a table or a better example.
- Queries you never planned for: a new section, or a new article.

If Search Console is not set up yet, my guide on {{< a key="google-search-console-tutorial" >}}how to use Google Search Console{{< /a >}} walks you through it.

Search Console counts clicks, not clients. Enquiries arrive by form and by phone, so ask every new client how they found you, and which article they read if they remember. I also use Microsoft Clarity to see where visitors engage and which parts make them leave, so I know whether to fix the copy, a picture or the layout.

Give an article a few months before you judge it, and update it whenever a fact changes: a price, a rule, a product.

## How to write SEO blog posts: the checklist before you publish

1. The topic is a question customers really ask, ideally close to a purchase.
2. One main keyword, checked in a keyword tool, that no other page of your site targets.
3. The answer sits in the first two or three sentences.
4. Each H2 answers one question; steps are numbered and comparisons are in tables.
5. The article holds at least one thing only your business knows.
6. Every fact is checked, and no [TO CONFIRM] is left.
7. The article links to the page that solves the problem, and that page shows a phone number.
8. The title, meta description, URL and alt text use the words people search for.
