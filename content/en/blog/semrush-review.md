Semrush is an online suite of SEO and advertising research tools: keyword research, competitor analysis, site audits, rank tracking and more. My answer to "is Semrush worth it?" depends on how often you would open it. If you or your team do SEO work every week, on several websites or for clients, the paid plans earn their place. If you run one website with a handful of service pages, the free account and Google's free tools cover most of what you need.

I use Semrush mainly for keyword research, and for one thing in particular: the keywords advertisers pay for, and what each click costs them. That cost shows which keywords bring business. I then go after those keywords with SEO, so the business gets the visits without paying for every click.

This review walks through that workflow, what Semrush costs at the time of writing (October 2026), when a paid plan makes sense, and the cheaper alternatives. It is part of my guide to {{< a key="small-business-tools-guide" >}}small business marketing tools{{< /a >}}, the stack I use every day.

## What I actually use Semrush for

Semrush does far more than keyword research: site audits, rank tracking, competitor research, content tools, ad research and, more recently, visibility in AI search. I use it mainly for two things, and they work together.

### Keyword research: what people type, and how often

The core of my use is keyword research: finding the words people type into Google when they need what a business sells, and how often they type them. For each phrase, Semrush's **Keyword Overview** shows:

- **Volume**: the average number of searches a month, in the country you choose and worldwide.
- **Keyword Difficulty (KD%)**: a score from 0 to 100 for how hard it is to reach Google's top 10.
- **Intent**: what the person searching most likely wants, from information to a purchase.
- **CPC**: the average price an advertiser pays Google for one click on an ad for that keyword.
- **Trend**: how searches for it move over the year.

### Ad keywords, and what each click costs

The CPC (cost per click) is the figure I look at most. Advertisers rarely keep paying for clicks that bring them nothing, so a keyword with a high CPC is usually one that brings customers. A high CPC also suggests stronger buying intent, and more competition for it.

That is how I decide where the SEO effort goes. When a keyword is expensive in ads, ranking for it in the organic results brings the same kind of visitor without a bill for each click. **I double down on those keywords with SEO instead of paying for them in ads.** My comparison of {{< a key="seo-vs-ppc" >}}SEO vs PPC{{< /a >}} explains when paying for the ads still makes sense, for instance while the page climbs.

## My workflow in the free account: 10 requests a day

A free Semrush account allows 10 requests a day, according to [Semrush's plan comparison](https://www.semrush.com/kb/856-subscription-plans-comparison), and each report shows only its first 10 results. That is tight, but enough to check a short list properly, as long as you prepare the list before you log in.

1. Write the list first. Note the words your clients use on the phone, in emails and in reviews, and keep the ten that describe what you sell most directly. Every search counts towards the ten, so vague ideas waste them.
2. Choose the country. Semrush keeps separate data for each country it covers: pick the United Kingdom for a British business, or your clients' country if you sell abroad.
3. Check each phrase in Keyword Overview. Copy the volume, KD%, CPC and intent into a spreadsheet, one row per phrase.
4. Run your best phrase through the **Keyword Magic Tool**. It turns one seed phrase into a list of related searches that you can filter by volume, difficulty and CPC. The free account shows only the first 10, so start from a precise phrase such as "boiler repair Leeds" rather than "boiler".
5. Shortlist the specific, expensive keywords. Keep the phrases with real volume, a high CPC and a difficulty your site can realistically reach. Specific beats broad: of the websites I built for one client, the one selling very specific services ranked, while the one that offered everything never did.
6. Give each keyword its own page. One primary keyword per page, in the title, the address, the first paragraph and one heading. Then watch that page's impressions in Google Search Console over the following weeks.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}Ten requests go fast when you search on impulse. Prepare the list the evening before, and **keep the next day's ten for the variations today's searches revealed**.{{< /callout >}}

For a second opinion on what advertisers pay, Google Keyword Planner shows its own bid ranges for free; I come back to it below. The full method, from the first list of words to the plan of your pages, is in my guide to {{< a key="keyword-research-small-business" >}}keyword research for small businesses{{< /a >}}.

## Semrush prices in October 2026

Semrush sells its tools as separate toolkits. The one for keyword research is the **SEO Toolkit**, in three plans, and **Semrush One** bundles it with the AI Visibility Toolkit. These are the prices at the time of writing (October 2026):

| Plan | Billed monthly | Billed annually | What sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free account | $0 | $0 | 10 requests a day, 10 results per report |
| SEO Toolkit Pro | $139.95 a month | $117.33 a month | Semrush's plan for freelancers, small businesses and small teams |
| SEO Toolkit Guru | $249.95 a month | $208.33 a month | Adds, among other things, historical data back to January 2012 |
| SEO Toolkit Business | $499.95 a month | $416.66 a month | The highest limits of the three |
| Semrush One | From just under $200 a month | From $165.17 a month | The SEO Toolkit plus the AI Visibility Toolkit; Pro+ costs $299 and Advanced $549 a month |

Semrush lists these prices in US dollars, so I give no £ figure here: check the final amount, with any VAT, at checkout. The current offer is on [Semrush's pricing page](https://www.semrush.com/prices/), and plans and prices change, so look there before you subscribe.

Over a year, Pro comes to about $1,680 billed monthly, or about $1,408 billed annually. That is the figure to weigh against what the keywords could bring you.

## Is Semrush worth it for your business?

Semrush suits people who do SEO work every week. Freelancers, agencies and in-house marketers who publish content regularly, track rankings across several sites or keep an eye on competitors are likely to open it most days. For them it is a working tool, like a van for a plumber.

It suits a small business owner less well. With one website and a handful of service pages, you need keyword research when you plan the site, when you add a page, and perhaps once a quarter to see where you stand. Between those moments, a paid plan sits unused.

| Your situation | What I would use |
|---|---|
| One local business website, a few service pages | The free account, plus Google's free tools |
| A new website, or a batch of new pages to plan | One month of Pro, billed monthly, then stop |
| Articles every week, rankings to track, competitors to watch | Pro, month after month |
| Several websites, or clients of your own | Pro or Guru, depending on the limits you need |
| You want to know whether AI assistants mention you | The free manual test first, Semrush One only to track it at scale |

A simple test: billed annually, Pro costs about $1,408 a year. If the keywords you find bring you one client a year who would not have found you otherwise, and that client is worth more than that, the subscription pays for itself. If you cannot picture that client, keep the free account.

{{< callout type="warning" >}}A tool this complete feels like progress. It is easy to fall for new shiny tools, but **the channels you already use are the ones that need your attention**. A paid plan you open once a month is a cost, not a strategy.{{< /callout >}}

## Cheaper Semrush alternatives

Google's free tools each cover part of the job, while Ahrefs and SE Ranking are full suites like Semrush, with a free or cheaper way in. The details below come from each company's own website, so test the tools on keywords you already know before you decide.

### Google's free tools

- **Google Keyword Planner** is free inside a Google Ads account. It gives monthly search volumes (as ranges if you are not spending on ads) and top-of-page bid ranges, which are Google's own signal of what advertisers pay. My {{< a key="google-keyword-planner-tutorial" >}}Google Keyword Planner tutorial{{< /a >}} shows how to use it without running ads.
- **Google Search Console** is free and shows the searches your site already appears for, with clicks, impressions and average position, straight from Google. It cannot show the searches you do not appear for yet, which is where Semrush or Keyword Planner come in. My {{< a key="google-search-console-tutorial" >}}Google Search Console guide{{< /a >}} covers the set-up.
- **Google Trends** is free too, and handy for spotting seasonal peaks and for comparing two ways of saying the same thing.

### Ahrefs and its free Webmaster Tools

Ahrefs is the best-known alternative to Semrush. Its free Ahrefs Webmaster Tools give you Site Explorer, Site Audit and Web Analytics for the websites you own, once you have verified them: enough to see your own site's backlinks and technical issues without paying. The paid plans start at $29 a month with Starter, and the next plan up, Lite, costs $129 a month at the time of writing, according to [Ahrefs' pricing page](https://ahrefs.com/pricing).

### SE Ranking

SE Ranking is another all-in-one SEO suite. At the time of writing, its website lists a Core plan at $129 a month and a Growth plan at $279, with a 14-day free trial. At the entry level, that is close to Semrush Pro, so compare what each plan includes rather than the headline price.

## What to do next, and when to hand it over

You do not need an agency to get started. Here is the order I would follow:

1. Open a free Semrush account and, if you have not already, add your site to Google Search Console.
2. Write down ten phrases your clients use, and check them in Keyword Overview.
3. Shortlist three keywords: specific, searched for, and expensive in ads.
4. Plan one page per keyword, or improve the page that already targets it.
5. If you keep hitting the 10-request limit for weeks, try Pro for one month, billed monthly, before you commit to a year.
6. A month or two after publishing, open Search Console and check whether those pages get impressions for their keywords.
