A new tool, platform or tactic arrives every month, each one promising more clients than what you already do. Shiny object syndrome is the habit of following each of them in turn, and marketing is full of it. Small businesses pay the most for it, because they have the least time to spare.

I do not have grand theories about marketing: it can be annoying, but every business needs it. One thing I am sure of, though: it is easy to fall for new shiny tools and practices, when what pays is to focus on the channels you already run and make them thrive. There is a lot of noise in marketing. That noise is the point, and owners of small businesses and small teams are easy to catch with it.

My guide on {{< a key="how-to-get-more-clients" >}}how to get more clients{{< /a >}} compares the channels themselves. This article answers the next question: how many to run at once, and when to add another.

## What shiny object syndrome looks like in a small business

The name sounds dramatic, but the pattern is ordinary: the next idea always looks better than the current one, so nothing runs long enough to work. In a small business, it rarely looks reckless. It looks like keeping up.

You probably have it if several of these sound familiar:

- You have accounts on more platforms than you post on.
- You pay for tools you have not opened this month.
- Your plan changes after every webinar, newsletter or viral post.
- You cannot say which channel brought in your last three clients.
- You stopped your SEO, your ads or your emails before they could work, because something newer came along.
- Each quarter starts with a new platform, and none reaches its third month.

The problem is rarely a lack of channels. It is **the lack of time each channel gets**.

## Why marketing makes it so easy to catch

Every platform, tool and course competes for your attention, exactly as you compete for your customers'. The 2026 edition of the [martech map published on chiefmartec](https://chiefmartec.com/2026-marketing-technology-landscape/), Scott Brinker's blog, counts 15,505 marketing software products. In one year, 1,488 products joined the map and 1,367 dropped off it, and each newcomer is sold on the idea that what you use today is not enough.

## What chasing every new channel really costs

The subscription is the smallest part of the bill. Each new channel brings a set-up, a learning curve and a wait before the first results, and each switch sends you back to the start of all three. I have paid that learning cost myself: with Wix, I learnt a new platform, saw it removed, and had to learn again.

What you lose most is compounding. A channel gets better the longer you run it, because you learn what works:

- Cold emailing: I A/B test the copy, and over time a campaign usually gets more replies, since I know which wording worked and which did not. The third email also brings positive replies: busy people had forgotten the first two, or had no time to answer them. Stop after two emails and you miss those replies.
- SEO: pages keep working after you publish them, and each new one supports the others. Abandon them after six weeks and you leave before the results arrive.
- Pinterest and YouTube Shorts: every post you add joins the ones already bringing views.

This is what mastering one channel looks like. ## How to overcome shiny object syndrome: master one channel, then add the next

The cure is not willpower: it is a routine you decide in advance. What follows is general advice, not a formula built on my own figures, and it works with any channel.

### 1. List the channels you already run

Write one line per channel, including the ones you have neglected:

| Column | What to write |
|---|---|
| Channel | Referrals, Google, cold emailing, Instagram, a newsletter… |
| Enquiries | How many came from it in the last three months |
| Clients | How many of those enquiries became paying clients |
| Cost | The money spent, plus the hours a week it takes |
| Verdict | Keep, improve or stop |

{{< callout type="tip" >}}Ask every new enquiry how they found you and **write the answer down**. Without it, you are guessing which channel works.{{< /callout >}}

### 2. Improve one channel at a time

Pick the channel that brings the most clients for the hours it takes, or the one closest to working. Change one thing at a time, such as the offer, the first line of an email, a page title or the day you post, and give each change long enough to show a result. Keep the other channels on a simple routine in the meantime: maintained, not reinvented.

### 3. Decide what "runs without you" means

A channel runs without you when it no longer needs your daily attention: the steps are written down, the routine is scheduled or handed to a person or a tool, and the results hold for a few months without you rescuing them. Until then, a new channel steals hours from the one that pays.

### 4. Test the next channel with an end date and a stop rule

Before you start, write down four things: the goal, such as a number of enquiries; the budget, in money and in hours; the end date; and the result that will make you stop. Then leave the plan alone until the end date, whatever new tool appears in the meantime.

How long to give a channel depends on how it works:

| Channel | Judge it after | Stop rule to set in advance |
|---|---|---|
| Cold emailing | The full sequence (three emails at most) sent to a clean list; in my campaigns, the first result usually comes within 36 hours | The number of replies or calls you need from that list |
| SEO | Months, not weeks: four months to a year, according to [Google's guidance on hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) | The pages and searches you expect to move by the end date |
| Paid ads (Google, Meta, LinkedIn) | The test budget, spent in full | The highest cost per enquiry you can afford |
| Pinterest and YouTube Shorts | Several months of regular posting | The visits or enquiries you expect by the end date |

Paid ads give the fastest answer, but they burn cash fast, LinkedIn Ads especially, so the budget itself becomes your stop rule. SEO is the opposite: judge it too early and you stop just before it works, as my {{< a key="how-long-does-seo-take" >}}honest SEO timeline{{< /a >}} explains.

{{< callout type="warning" >}}Do not end a test halfway because something new caught your eye. **Finish it, then decide.**{{< /callout >}}

## Before you add a new channel: a checklist

Run through these questions before you sign up for anything new:

1. Did I need this before I saw it?
2. Does it reach the buyers I already sell to?
3. What will I stop doing to make time for it?
4. What does it really cost: the price, plus set-up, learning and upkeep?
5. When will I judge it, and what result will make me stop?
6. Does my current channel run without me yet?

If the last answer is no, the new channel can wait. Put the idea on a list, look at it again at your next review, and spend the hour on the channel that already pays. A channel with a written routine and a clear target is also the easiest to hand over, to an employee, a freelancer or an agency, on the day you want your time back.
