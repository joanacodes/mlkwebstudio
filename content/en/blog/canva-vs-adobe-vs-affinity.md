For most small businesses, the Canva vs Adobe question has a short answer: Canva covers the visuals you make every week, and a professional tool is only worth adding when a specific job needs it. Since October 2025, that professional tool can cost nothing, because Canva relaunched Affinity as a free app.

This is how I split the work myself. I use Canva every day for little tasks: not having Canva for my work would be like not having a kettle in the house. For heavy work, I go for Affinity or the Adobe suite.

Below, I compare the three on what they do best, how they charge, which tool suits which job and the mistakes I would avoid. Prices change often, so rather than quote figures that may be out of date next month, I explain how each one bills and link to the official pages. This comparison is part of my guide to {{< a key="small-business-tools-guide" >}}the marketing tools a small business actually needs{{< /a >}}.

## Canva vs Adobe vs Affinity at a glance

Canva is an online design app built on templates. Adobe Creative Cloud is the subscription suite most professional designers work in: Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign and many others. Affinity is a professional desktop app that combines vector, photo and layout tools; Canva has owned it since 2024 and made it free in October 2025.

| | Canva | Affinity | Adobe Creative Cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best for | Social posts, Pins, flyers, presentations | Logos, brand identities, print layouts, photo editing | The same heavy work, in the formats agencies and designers use |
| Learning curve | Gentle: templates do most of the work | Steep: a professional tool | Steep, and each app is a separate tool to learn |
| How you pay | Free plan, then paid plans per person or per team | Free; the Canva AI tools inside it need a paid Canva plan | Monthly subscription, usually with a one-year commitment |
| Where it runs | Browser, plus apps for computer and phone | Mac and Windows | Mac and Windows; some apps on iPad and the web |
| Your files | Stay in your Canva account; you download PDF, PNG, JPG and more | .af files saved on your computer | .psd, .ai and .indd, the files designers exchange |

The short version: Canva for the daily visuals, Affinity for the occasional heavy job once you are ready to learn it, Adobe when your work depends on Adobe files.

## What Canva does well, and where it stops

Canva works from templates. You choose a format, such as an Instagram post, a Pinterest Pin, an A5 flyer or a presentation, then change the text, colours and photos and download the result. It runs in a browser and in apps for computer and phone, and a team can share the same designs and folders.

For small daily things, **Canva is perfect**. That covers most of what a small business produces in a typical week:

- social media posts and stories, which you can then plan ahead with one of the {{< a key="social-media-scheduling-tools" >}}social media scheduling tools{{< /a >}} I compare elsewhere;
- Pinterest Pins, one of the few channels where a post keeps working for months, as I explain in my guide to {{< a key="pinterest-for-business" >}}Pinterest for business{{< /a >}};
- flyers, menus, posters and business cards for the printer;
- presentations, proposals and simple PDF documents;
- quick image fixes: cropping, resizing, adjusting the brightness.

Canva stops being the right tool in three cases. A logo built from a template is, by definition, available to everyone else who opens that template. Canva says so itself: logos made from its templates are not exclusive and cannot be registered as a trademark. If you design one from scratch in Canva, use only fonts and simple lines and shapes, not the photos and graphics of its library ([Canva's help page on logos and trademarks](https://www.canva.com/en_gb/help/trademarks-logo/)). Detailed photo work, such as retouching a product shot or combining several photos into one, needs the precision of a photo editor. And a long print document, a 40-page catalogue for instance, is easier to build in a layout tool that lets text flow from one page to the next.

The free plan goes a long way. The paid plans add features such as brand kits, one-click resizing and a larger premium library, and the list changes from year to year. What the upgrade costs, and whether it pays for itself, is a separate question, which I answer in {{< a key="canva-pro-review" >}}is Canva Pro worth it{{< /a >}}.

## When Adobe is worth paying for

Adobe Creative Cloud is where most professional designers work: Photoshop for photos, Illustrator for logos and vector graphics, InDesign for multi-page layouts, plus apps for video and PDF. Its file formats, .psd, .ai and .indd, are what agencies and freelance designers send each other.

For a small business, Adobe earns its price in two situations:

1. Someone on your team designs most days and needs the depth: advanced retouching, compositing, typography, video.
2. You exchange working files with designers or agencies that use Adobe. If an agency builds your brand in Illustrator, you need Illustrator to edit those source files properly.

Outside those cases, buying Creative Cloud 'because professionals use it' is the design version of the **shiny-object trap**. It is easy to fall for a new tool when the ones you already have would do the job, and a subscription you rarely open still costs you every month.

### How Adobe's plans work (October 2026)

On Adobe's UK site, the individual plans include Creative Cloud Pro and Creative Cloud Standard, which both give you the desktop apps such as Photoshop and Illustrator. Standard costs less but comes with only a small allowance of Adobe's generative AI, so if you do not need AI image generation, compare Standard with the single-app plans, such as Photoshop on its own, before you pick Pro. Prices are shown including VAT. The lower monthly prices come with a one-year commitment, paid monthly or upfront; the plan you can stop in any month costs more.

Check two things before you pay. Introductory offers on the first months are common, so look at the price you will pay once the offer ends. And read the cancellation terms shown at checkout, because ending an annual plan early is not always free. The current figures are on [Adobe's plans page](https://www.adobe.com/uk/creativecloud/plans.html).

Adobe also has its own answer to Canva, Adobe Express. If your business already pays for Creative Cloud, try Express before you add a Canva subscription on top.

## Affinity: the professional option that is now free

Affinity was built by Serif, a British software company, as a pay-once alternative to Adobe's subscriptions, sold as three apps: Designer for vector work, Photo for images, Publisher for layouts. Canva bought it in 2024 and, in October 2025, relaunched it as a single app that is free for everyone. You switch between three studios, Vector, Pixel and Layout, inside one workspace ([Canva's announcement](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/all-new-affinity/)).

What is free and what is not, at the time of writing (October 2026):

- The app and its professional tools are free, on Mac and Windows. You sign in with a Canva account, which can be a free one. An iPad version was announced for 2026: the download page shows whether it has arrived.
- Canva's AI tools inside Affinity, such as Generative Fill and image or vector generation, need a Canva premium plan: Pro, Business, Enterprise or Education.
- If you bought Affinity V2, your licence still works, but the V2 apps no longer receive updates. The details are on the [Affinity download page](https://www.affinity.studio/get-affinity).

{{< callout type="tip" >}}If you already pay for Canva Pro, the **Canva AI tools in Affinity** come with your plan. Moving up from Canva then costs you time to learn, not another subscription.{{< /callout >}}

Affinity suits someone who needs a logo, a brand identity, a print-ready brochure or proper photo editing a few times a year, and does not want a monthly subscription for it. The catch is the learning curve. Affinity is a professional tool built to rival Adobe's apps, so plan to learn it like one, not like Canva: a free tool you never find the time to learn is not a saving.

It suits you less if your designers or your agency send you Adobe source files every week. In that case, stay where they work.

## Which tool for which job

| Job | Best fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Social posts and stories | Canva | Templates in the right sizes, ready in minutes |
| Pinterest Pins | Canva | Pin-sized templates, easy to make in batches |
| Flyers, menus, business cards | Canva, or Affinity for strict print specifications | Canva exports print PDFs; Affinity gives full control over bleed and colour |
| Presentations and proposals | Canva | Easy to share and edit as a team |
| Logo and visual identity | Affinity or Adobe Illustrator | A vector file built from scratch, which you own |
| Product or portrait retouching | Affinity (Pixel studio) or Photoshop | Layers, masks and precise selections |
| Brochure or catalogue of many pages | Affinity (Layout studio) or InDesign | Text that flows across pages, print-ready PDF |
| Source files from an agency in Adobe formats | Adobe | No conversion, no surprises |

If most of your jobs land in the Canva rows, that is normal. For many small businesses, a design tool's job is to produce clean, consistent visuals every week, not to build a brand from scratch every month.

## How I use them: Canva daily, Affinity or Adobe for heavy work

The split is simple: little tasks go to Canva, heavy work to Affinity or the Adobe suite. In a website project, the heavy design work is the visual identity, and it comes first.

I ask about the company, what it offers and the clients it prefers working with, then I browse a lot of libraries for visual identity, create one, show it to the client and edit it if necessary. Only then do I move on to the design templates, the SEO research and the copy. I describe the whole sequence in {{< a key="web-design-process" >}}my web design process{{< /a >}}.

The aim is that my clients look good. I want their prospects to take them seriously, even to be slightly intimidated by the website, which also tends to reduce the haggling over price. A logo that any competitor could have picked from the same template does not create that effect: an identity is worth building from scratch. There is more on this in {{< a key="professional-looking-website" >}}how to make your website look professional{{< /a >}}.

For {{< a page="/projects/eden-flats" >}}Eden Flats{{< /a >}}, an Airbnb property management company in Paris and the Île-de-France, the visual branding came with the new website and the SEO, and owners started calling to have their properties managed.

Once an identity exists, the daily visuals can go back to Canva: the logo files, colours and fonts set the rules, and Canva applies them every day. That is the split I recommend to most small businesses: a professional tool, or a professional, for the foundations, and Canva for everything else.

## Mistakes to avoid when choosing design software

- Paying for Adobe to feel professional. Your customers see the result, not the software. Subscribe when a person or a job needs it.
- Turning a template into your logo. It is fine for a test or a side project, and a poor foundation for a brand you will put on your vans, your quotes and your website for years.
- Learning three tools at once. Canva plus one professional tool is plenty: pick Affinity or Adobe, not both, unless your work demands it.
- Scattering your brand files. Keep one folder with your logo (vector and PNG versions), your colour codes and your font names, and share it with anyone who designs for you.
- Treating 'free' as 'no cost'. Affinity costs nothing to install, but it takes time to learn: start a few weeks before the job is due, not the night before.

## Where to start: a five-step plan

1. List the visuals your business makes in a typical month: posts, Pins, flyers, quotes, presentations.
2. Make them with Canva's free plan for a month. Upgrade only if a paid feature would save you time every week.
3. Install Affinity the day a heavy job appears, such as a logo, a print brochure or a photo that needs real retouching, and give yourself time to learn it.
4. Subscribe to Adobe only when a person on your team, a designer or an agency needs Adobe files, and choose the smallest plan that covers the apps you actually open.
5. If you would rather not learn a professional tool, hand the identity to a designer, and keep the daily visuals in-house, in Canva.

A professional identity built once, plus a simple tool you open every day, is a combination most small businesses can afford and keep up.
