Pinterest for business is one of the two channels I recommend to small businesses that have no time to feed an algorithm every day (the other is YouTube Shorts). People go to Pinterest to search for ideas, and a good Pin keeps turning up in those searches for months, sending visitors to your website long after you published it.

But if you asked me where to spend the few hours you can give to social media, and your work is visual, my answer for 2026 would be Pinterest. ## Why Pinterest for business works like a search engine

Many people open Pinterest with a project in mind: a kitchen to redo, a wedding to organise, a garden to plant, a dinner to cook. They type what they want, save the ideas they like and click through to the pages that explain how to get the result. That is search behaviour, and there is a lot of it: in its results published on 4 August 2026, Pinterest reported a record 640 million monthly active users worldwide for the second quarter ([Pinterest investor relations](https://investor.pinterestinc.com/)).

Search changes the life of a post. On Instagram, TikTok or Facebook, a post usually gets most of its reach in its first days, then you need another one. On Pinterest, a Pin is matched to searches and to people's interests, so it can keep appearing, and keep being saved, months after you published it. Followers also matter less than elsewhere, because people find Pins through search and recommendations rather than by following accounts.

That is why I see Pinterest and YouTube Shorts as probably your best allies in 2026: what you post there is evergreen. These two platforms work for you, not the other way round. Instagram, TikTok and Facebook make you an unpaid employee who has to keep begging for attention; even {{< a key="google-business-profile-posts" >}}Google Business Profile posts{{< /a >}} are kinder to your time. The other half of that advice is in my guide to {{< a key="youtube-shorts-for-business" >}}YouTube Shorts for business{{< /a >}}.

## Which businesses Pinterest suits, and which it barely helps

Pinterest rewards businesses whose results can be photographed and whose customers plan before they buy.

| Fit | Examples | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Interior design, kitchens and bathrooms, renovation, gardens and landscaping, weddings and events, florists, bakers and caterers, fashion, jewellery, crafts, home goods, online shops | People search for ideas, save them and come back to buy or book |
| Possible, with effort | Hairdressers and beauty salons, photographers, architects, holiday lets, coaches who publish step-by-step guides | The result is visual, but many of the people who save it live far from you |
| Barely | Accountants, solicitors, IT support, emergency repairs, most B2B services sold over a call | Nobody saves a picture of a tax return or a repaired boiler |

The quick test: would someone save a picture of what you do to look at it again later? If so, Pinterest can work for you. If you work locally, keep in mind that Pinterest's audience goes far beyond your town: Pins work best when they point to something people can buy, book or use from anywhere, such as a product, a guide or a portfolio.

If your business sits in the last row, do not open a Pinterest account because a blog told you to. If there is one thing I insist on in marketing, it is this: it is easy to fall for shiny new tools and channels, and a small business gets more from focusing on the ones it already runs.

## Set up a free Pinterest business account and claim your website

1. **Create the account.** Go to [Pinterest Business](https://business.pinterest.com/) and create a business account, or convert your personal one. It costs nothing and adds Pinterest's analytics and access to ads.
2. **Fill in the profile.** Your business name, a clear logo or photo, a two-line description that says what you do and for whom, in your customers' words, and your website address.
3. **Claim your website.** Open the menu at the top right, go to **Settings**, then **Claimed accounts**, and click **Claim** next to **Websites**. You prove the site is yours with an HTML tag pasted into the `<head>` of your pages or an HTML file uploaded to the root of your site; some accounts can also add a {{< a key="dns-records-explained" >}}TXT record{{< /a >}} to the domain's DNS instead. You need to own the domain and be able to edit the site. Once the site is claimed, your analytics also cover the Pins other people create from your pages. [Pinterest's help page on claiming a website](https://help.pinterest.com/en/business/article/claim-your-website) details each method.
4. **Create your first boards.** Five to ten boards, each named after something your customers search ("Small kitchen ideas", "Garden lighting"), not after your internal service names, with a one-sentence description.

The HTML tag looks like this, with the code Pinterest gives you:

```html
<meta name="p:domain_verify" content="your-code-from-pinterest"/>
```

{{< callout type="tip" >}}Most website builders have a field for verification codes or for code in the page header, sometimes only on paid plans. If you cannot find it and Pinterest offers you the **TXT record**, add that in your domain's DNS settings instead: DNS changes can take up to 72 hours to apply.{{< /callout >}}

## Find the words people search with Pinterest Trends

Pinterest is a search engine, so the words on your Pins and boards decide who sees them. Two free places show you the words people actually use:

- **Pinterest Trends** ([trends.pinterest.com](https://trends.pinterest.com/)): type a term and see how its searches rise and fall across the year, with a filter for your country when it is covered. It tells you when people start looking for "Christmas table ideas" or "garden party", and that is when your Pins should already be live.
- **Pinterest's search bar**: type the start of your topic and note the suggestions. They come from real searches and are often more specific than the ones you would have guessed.

Then write the way your customers search, not the way a brochure talks. "Bespoke joinery solutions" is how a business describes itself; "built-in wardrobe ideas" is what people type. Put the search phrase in the board name, in the Pin title, in the first line of the description and on the image itself.

Seasonal topics need a head start. People plan weddings, Christmas and garden projects well in advance, so publish those Pins several weeks before the date, not the week before.

## Make Pins in Canva that people want to click

I use Canva every day for small visuals: working without it would be like living in a house without a kettle. For Pins, it is all you need, and its Pinterest Pin template already has the size Pinterest recommends, a vertical 2:3 format of 1000 × 1500 pixels. For heavier design work I switch to Affinity or Adobe; my comparison of {{< a key="canva-vs-adobe-vs-affinity" >}}Canva, Adobe and Affinity{{< /a >}} explains when each one makes sense.

Here is what I would put on every Pin:

1. **A real photo of your work** as the main image: the finished kitchen, the bouquet, the plated dish.
2. **A short headline on the image** that repeats the search phrase and promises what the page delivers, such as "7 small kitchen layouts that work".
3. **Text you can read on a phone**: large type, strong contrast, one or two short lines.
4. **Your logo or domain, small**, at the bottom: enough to be recognised, without turning the Pin into an advert.
5. **A title and a description** that start with the search phrase and read as plain sentences.
6. **A link to the exact page** the Pin talks about, not to your home page.

Make several Pins for each page you want to promote, with different photos and headlines. Each new Pin is another chance to match a search, and after a few weeks your analytics show which angle people click.

## Which pages your Pins should send visitors to

A Pin is a promise, and the click has to land on a page that keeps it. These pages work best:

- **Guides and blog posts** that answer a planning question, such as how to choose kitchen worktops. The same articles serve Google and Pinterest: my guide on how to {{< a key="write-seo-blog-posts" >}}write SEO blog posts{{< /a >}} shows how to plan them.
- **Project or portfolio pages**, one per project, with several photos.
- **Product pages** with the price and availability visible.
- **Seasonal pages**: a Christmas menu, a wedding package, spring garden services.

Check each page on a phone before you pin it: it should load quickly and show the image from the Pin near the top of the screen. To see these visits in your own analytics, add tracking parameters to the link you give Pinterest:

```text
https://www.yourdomain.co.uk/small-kitchen-layouts/?utm_source=pinterest&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=small-kitchen
```

## How often to pin, and when to expect visitors

I would rather see you publish two or three new Pins a week for six months than twenty in one weekend followed by silence. Choose a rhythm you can keep, prepare a month of Pins in one sitting and schedule them. I like Metricool for that: besides scheduling, it lets you watch what successful competitors publish and follow your own growth. My comparison of {{< a key="social-media-scheduling-tools" >}}social media scheduling tools{{< /a >}} puts it side by side with Postly and Buffer.

Then be patient. A new Pin takes time to be found, saved and shown to more people, so judge Pinterest in months, not days. The figure that matters is **outbound clicks** in Pinterest's analytics, meaning the clicks that leave Pinterest for your site, followed by the visits and enquiries they bring in your own analytics. Impressions only tell you the Pins are being seen, and follower counts tell you even less.

## What Pinterest's AI labels mean for your Pins

Two changes made in 2025 affect how people see your images:

- **The "AI modified" label.** Pinterest marks image Pins it detects as generated or edited with AI, using the image's metadata and its own classifiers. The label sits in the bottom-left corner when someone opens the Pin. Detection is not perfect: a real photo retouched with a generative AI tool can get the label too, and you can appeal a label you think is wrong.
- **Fewer AI Pins on request.** From the three-dot menu on a Pin, or in their settings, people can ask to see fewer AI Pins in categories such as beauty, art, fashion and home decor.

The practical consequence is the advice above: photos of your real work are your best material, especially in those categories. Canva is fine for the layout, but an image generated or edited with generative AI may carry the label, and part of your audience may have asked to see fewer of them.

## Your first month on Pinterest: a checklist

1. **Week 1:** create the business account, complete the profile, claim your website and create five to ten boards.
2. **Week 2:** list 15 to 20 search phrases from Pinterest Trends and the search bar, and pick the five pages of your site you want to promote.
3. **Week 3:** make three Pins per page in Canva, with titles, descriptions and tracked links.
4. **Week 4:** schedule them over the following weeks, then block an hour each week to make new ones.
5. **After three months:** compare outbound clicks and visits page by page, and make new Pins for the pages that work.
